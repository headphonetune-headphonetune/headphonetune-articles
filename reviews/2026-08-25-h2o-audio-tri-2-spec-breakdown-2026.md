The core proposition: a true dual-mode headphone with an 8GB MP3 player The most striking thing about the TRI 2 is that it doesn't rely solely on Bluetooth. It has a built-in MP3 player with 8GB of storage. That's not a gimmick; it's a necessity. Bluetooth signals don't travel well through water, and the spec sheet is upfront about it: Bluetooth doesn't work underwater. So if you're swimming laps, your phone on the deck is useless. The 8GB internal memory solves that problem. You load your music onto the headphones themselves, switch to MP3 mode, and you're good to go for the entire swim session. Eight gigabytes is a meaningful amount of storage. For everyday listening. The listing claims it holds "several thousands of songs," which is plausible if you're using compressed formats like MP3 or M4A. It even accepts iTunes.M4A files. Which is a thoughtful touch for Apple users who don't want to re-encode their libraries. The practical upshot is that you can load a solid chunk of your music collection and leave your phone in the locker. That's a real advantage for pool swimmers who don't want to fiddle with a phone in a waterproof pouch or worry about dropping it in the deep end. The trade-off here's the transfer process. You'll need to plug the headphones into a computer via USB to load music. There's no wireless transfer, no cloud sync, no app that pulls from your streaming service. If you're someone who lives entirely in Spotify or Apple Music and hasn't owned A MP3 file in a decade, this will feel archaic. You'll have to source files. Organize them, and manually sync. Also,: the built-in MP3 player is the only way to get audio underwater. In real-world use, the Bluetooth mode is strictly for land-based activities like running, cycling, or gym workouts. That's not just sweatproof or splashproof; that's dive-ready for any recreational swimmer. Open water swimmers, lap swimmers, even snorkelers would be fine with this. That's great for situational awareness - you can hear traffic, other swimmers, or a coach's instructions. But it also means they don't block out ambient noise. Bone conduction is a compromise: you trade audio fidelity for the ability to hear your surroundings and to have something that survives underwater. If you're expecting bass-heavy, immersive sound like a pair of over-ear headphones, you'll be disappointed. If you're expecting clear, intelligible audio that lets you keep one ear on the environment, you'll be satisfied. Design and fit: open-ear bone conduction with a neckband form factor The TRI 2 uses a neckband design. The headphones wrap around the back of your head and rest on your cheekbones. The listing also mentions the bone conduction speakers are 5mm slimmer than the previous TRI model, which is a fine improvement for comfort, especially if you wear them under a swim cap or cycling helmet. Bone conduction works by vibrating your cheekbones, which transmits sound directly to your inner ear, bypassing the eardrum. This is why your ear canals stay open. The trade-off is that the sound quality is inherently limited by the physics of the technology. You won't get deep bass, and at high volumes, some users report a tickling or vibration sensation on the skin. The listing says the enhanced speakers give you "impressive sound in and out of water," but that's marketing language. What you should expect is clear mids and highs, adequate for podcasts, audiobooks, and most music genres, but not for bass-heavy electronic music or hip-hop where you want to feel the beat.
