Let's break down what you're actually getting with the Pro-10 model. Build & Materials The listing states a metal enclosure and a metal carrying case. That's a significant point in favor of durability. At 250 grams, they're not featherweights, but they're not heavy either. The ear cups are over-ear and swivel 90 degrees, which is a standard DJ attribute for single-ear monitoring-you can flip one cup back to listen to the room or a second source without taking the headphones off. The headband is described as adjustable and stretchable, which suggests it should accommodate different head sizes without much fuss. The padded ear cushions are mentioned for comfort and noise isolation. But note the "noise control" spec is listed as "Sound Isolation," not active noise canceling. This means they physically block some ambient sound by sealing around your ears, but they won't electronically cancel out a droning airplane engine or office chatter. For tracking in a studio or DJing at a moderate-volume gig, that's fine. For a noisy commute, it's a different story. Performance Expectations The headline spec is the 50-millimeter dynamic drivers with neodymium magnets. Larger drivers generally move more air, which translates to more authoritative bass. The marketing emphasizes "powerful bass," and the 110 dB sensitivity rating supports that. These will get loud without needing a solid amount of power from your source. The impedance is 32 ohms, which is low enough to be driven properly by a phone, laptop, or keyboard amp, but also high enough to work fine with a dedicated headphone amp or mixer. You won't need a separate amplifier to get usable volume, which is a practical advantage. In real-world use, the frequency response is listed oddly. One line says "40 KHz" and another says "20 Hz - 20,000 Hz." The latter is the standard human hearing range, and the former is likely a typo or a marketing claim about the driver's capability beyond human perception. Realistically, you should expect a response that covers the audible spectrum. The emphasis on bass means the tuning is likely V-shaped or at least bass-boosted. That's great for DJing, where you need to feel the kick drum, and for hip-hop or electronic music listening. For critical mixing and mastering. A boosted low end can be problematic-you might mix the bass too low on your final track because the headphones are flattering it. Many budget studio headphones do this, and the OneOdio is no exception. The spec sheet doesn't mention a flat response curve, so treat these as fun, energetic-sounding cans, not neutral reference monitors. The cable assembly is a strong point. It's a detachable, coiled DJ-style cord that stretches to 9.8 feet. That's plenty of reach to go from a mixer on a desk to a couch or bed. It terminates in both a 3.5mm and a 6.35mm plug, so you don't need an adapter for either a phone or a full-size mixer jack. Both plugs are detachable. Which is a thoughtful design-if you snag the cable and yank it, you're more likely to pull the cable out than break the headphone jack. The single-sided cable attachment also reduces tangling and weight on one side of your head. What Is Included The box includes the headphones, a detachable cable, a user manual, and a protective case. The case being metal is a notable inclusion at this price point. Most budget headphones come with a flimsy pouch or nothing at all. A rigid case means you can toss these in a gig bag without worrying about crushing them. For most people. So it's likely a no-frills cable without a mic or buttons. If you want to take calls or control playback from the cable, this probably isn't the right choice. The item weight is given as 250 grams, but that might be just the headphones without the cable and case. The coiled cable, which is thick, adds noticeable weight when carrying them. The carrying case material is listed as "Metal," which is unusual-most cases are hard plastic or fabric. It could be a metal-reinforced case or a typo. Either way, it's a rigid case, which is solid. On the practical side. The 77,096 customer reviews with a 4.4-star average is a strong signal. That's a massive sample size. You'll want to try them on if possible, or buy from a retailer with a good return policy. Real-World Usability Consider the intended use cases from the listing: DJing, mixing, podcasting, gaming, and monitoring. For DJing, the swiveling ear cups, coiled cable, and bass-heavy sound are all appropriate. You can cue tracks with one ear and hear the main mix with the other. The 9.8-foot cable gives you room to move around a booth. For podcasting. The sound isolation helps you hear yourself, but remember there's no built-in microphone. You'll need a separate mic. Which is standard for podcasting anyway. For gaming, the 3.5mm jack works with consoles and PCs, but the lack of a mic is a drawback if you play online multiplayer. You'd need a separate desk mic or a mod mic. For mixing. The bass boost is a double-edged sword. You'll enjoy listening to music, but you might not get a perfectly accurate picture of your mix. For monitoring in a recording session. They're fine-you'll hear the take clearly, and the isolation helps keep bleed out of the mic. The 32-ohm impedance means they work with almost anything. A phone. A laptop, a guitar amp, a keyboard, a DJ controller-all of these will drive them to adequate volume. For comparison, the sensitivity of 110 dB is high, so they'll get loud without pushing your source to max volume. This is a practical advantage if you're plugging into a device with a weak headphone jack. Build Quality Considerations The metal enclosure is a major plus. Many headphones in this price range creak and flex when you handle them. Metal suggests these will hold up to the twisting and bending that DJs put headphones through. The swiveling ear cups are a mechanical joint that can wear out over time, but with a metal construction, they're more likely to survive drops and rough handling. In daily use. The detachable cable is also a longevity feature-if the cable fails, you can replace it without replacing the headphones. That's a cost-saving measure in the long run. The Bass Question The marketing says "BASS SOUND" in all caps, and the 50mm drivers suggest they mean it. If you're looking for a neutral. Analytical sound to pick apart a mix, these are not it. If you want to feel the low end when you're listening to music or cueing up a track, these will deliver. The trade-off is that the midrange and treble might be recessed or less detailed compared to more expensive studio monitors. For the price. Yes. The headphones come with both a standard 6.35mm plug and a 3.5mm plug, and both are detachable, so you can connect directly to mixers, TVs, or devices with either jack size.

No, they do not have active noise cancellation. They offer sound isolation through the padded ear cushions. Which block some ambient noise passively, but they aren't water resistant either.
