Driver size and sound signature The 50mm dynamic drivers are larger than the 40mm units found on most headphones in this price tier. Bigger drivers don't automatically mean better sound. But they do mean more air moved, which typically translates to stronger bass presence and better headroom before distortion at higher volumes. The frequency response is listed as 20Hz-20kHz in one part of the spec sheet and 20Hz-40kHz in the technical specifications section. That inconsistency is worth noting. In real-world use, the 20kHz upper limit is standard for human hearing, so the 40kHz figure is either a typo or a marketing stretch. Either way. Don't buy these for ultrasonic frequencies. You won't hear them. What the 50mm drivers likely give you is a bass-forward signature. The listing emphasizes "dynamic bass" and "high-performance bass" repeatedly. Which suggests these are tuned for enjoyment rather than flat, clinical monitoring. That's not necessarily a below average thing. For DJ work, EDM production, or just listening to music with a lot of low-end energy, a bass boost can be flattering. For key mixing and mastering, you want neutrality. A headphone that hypes the low end will make your mixes sound thin when played back on other systems. The M20s sit somewhere in between - they're marketed as studio monitors. But the tuning leans toward consumer-friendly. If you're a professional engineer, that's a red flag. If you're a bedroom producer or a podcaster, it's probably fine. Impedance and sensitivity The 32-ohm impedance and 98dB sensitivity are the numbers that matter most for real-world use. A 32-ohm load is easy to drive. You can plug these into a phone. A laptop, a tablet, or a gaming controller and get adequate volume without a dedicated headphone amplifier. The 98dB sensitivity means they're efficient - they'll get loud with relatively little power. This combination makes them adaptable across devices. Which the long compatibility list confirms. The listing mentions mixers. Audio interfaces, electric guitars, synthesizers, keyboards, and even VCD/DVD players. That's a broad range, and the 32-ohm impedance is the reason why. You don't need to worry about whether your device can push them. The trade-off with low impedance is that these will pick up hiss from poorly designed headphone outputs. If you plug them into a budget laptop jack. You might hear background noise. That's not a flaw in the headphones - it's physics. But it's worth knowing.
