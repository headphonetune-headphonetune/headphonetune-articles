# Linsoul Kiwi Ears: Features &amp; Specs Guide

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/linsoul-kiwi-ears-features-specs-guide/)

The Kiwi Ears Altruva is a wired. Open-back, over-ear headphone aimed at people who care more about accurate sound than about noise isolation or convenience. It's a niche product - and the specs make that clear from the start. Let's walk through what each part of the spec sheet actually means for you if you're considering this. Open-Back Design: The Biggest Trade-Off The Altruva is open-back. That means the earcups have grilles or vents that let air pass through. The listing says this enhances soundstage and imaging, giving a "life-like width." That's accurate. Open-back headphones typically sound wider. More spacious, and more natural than closed-back ones because the drivers aren't working against a sealed chamber of air. Instruments get more separation. And the sound doesn't feel trapped inside your head. But here's the practical side. Open-back headphones leak sound both ways. People sitting next to you will hear what you're listening to. And you will hear them. The sound of a keyboard clacking, a conversation across the room, or traffic outside will bleed into your music. For everyday listening. The listing even mentions this is capable for "live streaming" and "podcasting," which is true - you can hear your own voice naturally, and the open design prevents that boomy, cupped-hands effect you get with closed headphones. But if you're in a shared office, on a commute, or in a room with a noisy roommate, this is a dealbreaker. There's no passive noise isolation to speak of. The spec sheet lists "Passive Noise Cancellation," but that's a misnomer here. Open-back headphones offer basically zero isolation. You're buying these for a quiet room, not for the bus. 50mm Dynamic Driver: What the Spacious Driver Gets You The Altruva uses a 50mm dynamic driver with neodymium magnets and a composite diaphragm made of PU and PEK with a copper ring. Larger drivers can move more air, which generally translates to better bass extension and higher volume headroom before distortion. The 50mm size is on the larger end for headphones. And it suggests the Altruva can produce a solid, physical thump in the low end if the tuning allows it. The listing claims the bass "hits hard and efficient" without bleeding into the mids. That's a common tuning goal - punchy, controlled bass rather than a warm, bloated low end. The midrange is described as natural and balanced, which is what you want for vocals and instruments. In real-world use, the treble is clear with "first-rate air," which means extended highs that add sparkle and detail rather than a dull, rolled-off top end. For a monitoring headphone. This is the right approach. You want to hear what's actually in the mix. Not what the headphones color. The 20Hz to 40kHz frequency response is wider than human hearing (which tops out around 20kHz for young people and less as you age). But that spec is mostly marketing. What matters is how the response is tuned in the audible range. And the description suggests a fairly neutral, studio-oriented curve. That means if you're an audio engineer, you can trust that a mix that sounds balanced on these will likely sound balanced on other neutral speakers. If you're a casual listener who likes bass-heavy consumer tuning (like a V-shaped curve), these might sound flat or boring to you. Impedance and Sensitivity: What You Need to Drive Them Impedance is 32 ohms, and sensitivity is 98 dB. Let's translate that. A 32-ohm load is low enough that most devices can drive them - a laptop headphone jack, a phone dongle, or a basic audio interface will get them to a reasonable volume. You don't need a dedicated headphone amplifier to make these work. That said, 98 dB sensitivity is moderate. For most people, it's not inefficient like some planar magnetic headphones that require serious power, but it's also not super-sensitive like a pair of IEMs designed for phones. Plugged into a weak laptop jack. You might find yourself pushing volume to 80% or higher. A budget USB dongle or a dedicated amp will give you more headroom and cleaner output, but it's not mandatory. For gaming, the low impedance means you can plug into a controller or a PC sound card without issue. The open-back nature will help with positional audio - footsteps and directional cues in games tend to be more precise on open-back headphones because the imaging is better. But again. You'll hear your own game audio in the room, and you'll hear your surroundings while playing. Materials and Build: Steel, Wood, and Vegan Leather The Altruva uses PVD-coated steel, wood, and vegan leather. PVD (physical vapor deposition) coating is a thin, hard finish that resists scratches and wear better than paint. Steel in the frame suggests durability - this isn't a flimsy plastic headphone. Wood earcups are a quality touch, but they're also a consideration for longevity. Wood can be affected by humidity and temperature over time. Though it's usually sealed. It adds weight and a certain aesthetic that some people love and others find gaudy. Vegan leather on the earpads is standard at this price point. It's softer and more breathable than pleather, but it will eventually wear and flake. Replacement pads are something to plan for down the line, though the listing doesn't specify whether they're user-swappable. The listing mentions "breathable and ergonomic," but it doesn't give a weight spec. That's a gap. A headphone with steel and wood construction could easily be 400 grams or more. For reference. Many well-fitting headphones weigh around 250-300 grams* (based on available data). If you're planning to wear these for long studio sessions or extended gaming marathons. Weight distribution matters more than the raw number. A well-balanced 400-gram headphone can feel lighter than a poorly balanced 300-gram one. Since the weight isn't listed, you're taking a chance on comfort. The over-ear design and the use of breathable materials suggest they've thought about it, but you won't know until you have them on your head. Wired Connection: Dual 3.5mm and What That Means The Altruva is wired-only. With a dual 3.5mm connection. That means each earcup has its own 3.5mm jack. And the cable splits to connect to both. This is a common design in audiophile headphones because it allows for balanced drive and easier cable replacement. The cable is included, and the box lists just "Cable" as the contents. No carrying case, no extra pads, no manual mentioned. That's minimal. For a headphone at this level, a case would be nice, but it's not unusual for budget-to-mid-fi audio gear to ship barebones. On the practical side. The dual 3.5mm jacks mean the cable is detachable. If you snag the cable and break a connection. You can replace just the cable rather than the whole headphone. That's a plus for longevity. But it also means you need to make sure the cable is fully seated in both earcups, and if you lose the stock cable, you need to buy one with the correct dual 3.5mm termination - not as universal as a single-sided 3.5mm or a 2.5mm* (based on available data) balanced connection. Noise Control: Passive, Which Means None The listing says "Passive Noise Cancellation." As mentioned, this is just the physical sealing of the earcups, which for open-back headphones is minimal. You will hear everything around you. For comparison. This isn't a flaw if you buy it for what it's - a monitoring tool for a quiet room. But if you're expecting any reduction in ambient noise, you'll be disappointed. There's no [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)we noticed , and the open-back design means you get the opposite of isolation. This is the single most important thing to understand before buying. Compatibility and Use Cases The listing mentions studio monitoring, podcasting, live streaming, and gaming. Let's break those down: - Studio monitoring: The neutral tuning and open-back design are great for key listening. You can trust what you hear. The 32-ohm impedance works with most audio interfaces. - Podcasting/live streaming: The open-back design lets you hear your own voice naturally, which helps you avoid speaking too loudly or too quietly. The lack of isolation means you'll hear your own mic monitoring if you have it set up, which is a plus for timing. - Gaming: Positional audio benefits from the open soundstage. But the lack of a microphone and the open-back design mean you need a separate mic. And you'll hear your own game audio bleeding out. Fine for a solo room, poor for a shared space. There's no built-in mic. No Bluetooth, no touch controls that matter (the listing says "control method: Touch," but with a wired cable, that likely refers to nothing or is a spec sheet error - ignore it). This is a pure, wired headphone. If you want to use it with a phone that lacks a headphone jack, you'll need a dongle. If you want wireless, look elsewhere. Kiwi Ears, is known primarily for IEMs (in-ear monitors) and has built a reputation for satisfactory value in the budget audiophile space. The Altruva is their foray into full-size over-ears. A 4.4 rating with that many reviews indicates it's not a dud, but it's also not a universally loved product. The warranty is one year, which is standard. What's Missing from the Specs There's no weight listed, no clamping force information, no headband padding details, and no mention of whether the earcups swivel or fold. The listing says "folding features: Over Ear," which is meaningless - it just describes the form factor, not that they fold. These are likely non-folding. Which makes them bulkier for transport. If you plan to travel with these, you'll need to carry them in a bag carefully. The lack of a case in the box reinforces that these are meant to sit on a desk, not go in a backpack. Also missing is any frequency response graph or distortion measurements. In daily use, the description tells you how they're tuned in general terms, but without a graph, you can't see if there are any peaks or dips in the treble that might make them harsh or sibilant. You're relying on the manufacturer's word and the aggregated customer reviews. Practical Day-to-Day Use Imagine using these at a desk. You plug them into your audio interface or a USB dongle. For typical buyers, the earpads are likely plush, but the weight - unknown but probably noticeable - will press down on your head over time. The sound is open and detailed. But then someone walks into the room and asks you a question. You hear them clearly because the headphones don't block anything. You pause your music, answer, and go back. In real-world use, the Kiwi Ears name doesn't carry the same weight in over-ears as it does in IEMs, so you're taking a bit of a risk on a newer product category for the brand. With 258 reviews, you can probably find those complaints if you dig through them. Who this is for This is for someone who has a quiet room, a wired configuration, and a priority on accurate sound. If you're an audio engineer mixing tracks, a musician checking your work, a podcaster who wants to hear your own voice naturally, or a gamer who plays alone in a quiet space and values positional audio over immersion, the Altruva is a strong candidate. You don't need an investment-grade amp to drive it. The tuning is described as neutral, and the open-back design gives you a soundstage that closed-back cans can't match.
