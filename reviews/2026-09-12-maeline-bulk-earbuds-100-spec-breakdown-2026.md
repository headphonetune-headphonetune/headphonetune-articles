The Maeline Bulk Earbuds 100 Pack is built for exactly that, and the spec sheet tells you fairly clearly what you're getting and, just as importantly, what you're not. What's actually in the box You get 100 pairs of wired in-ear earbuds. It comes in ten colors - pink, orange, yellow, red, white, grey, purple, black, blue, and green - which works out to ten pairs of each color. Each pair is individually packaged, and that detail matters more than it sounds. If you're handing these out to students or stocking a loaner drawer, individual packaging means you can distribute them one at a time without unspooling a tangled pile, and you can track how many you've given out against how many you have left. For everyday listening, it also means each pair arrives clean and sealed, which is the difference between a hygiene concern and a non-issue in a shared-use setting. There's no charging case, no cable, no adapter, no pouch. The box is earbuds and nothing else. The no-mic decision, and why it's the biggest thing to understand These earbuds have no microphone, no inline remote, and no volume control. The listing states this directly. And it's the single spec that should drive your decision. If you're buying these for students to take a video call, join a virtual class, or record audio, they won't do it. There's no mic to pick up a voice, so anything requiring two-way audio is off the table. That's not a defect - it's a design choice, and it has real upsides. Dropping the mic and remote strip out the parts that fail first on budget earbuds. Wires fray at the inline control. Buttons stop clicking, and the mic element is usually the first thing to go. Removing all of it leaves a simpler signal path with fewer solder points and fewer things to break. The seller frames this as focusing on audio and comfort, and mechanically that's a fair description. But you should read it as a hard limitation. Not a feature you can work around. No adapter or app adds a mic back. Fit and comfort The earpieces use rounded silicone tips in an in-ear design, and the listing describes them as light and ergonomic with soft silicone for extended wear. In-ear means the tip sits inside the ear canal, which does two things: it creates a physical seal that blocks some outside noise, and it can get uncomfortable after a while if the tip doesn't suit your ear shape. Silicone is the standard material here and generally tolerable for moderate stretches, but "extended wear" is doing some optimistic work. These are fine for a class period or a workout; they're not designed for a full transatlantic flight. In real-world use. The earpiece shape is listed as rounded tips, which is a common, unremarkable profile. There's no mention of multiple tip sizes. So you're getting one size per pair. For a bulk pack where fit has to work across a lot of different people. That's a real constraint - some users will find the seal loose and the bass thin, others will find them fine. It's the nature of one-size-fits-all earbuds. Noise isolation The spec sheet lists passive noise cancellation. That's a technical way of saying the earbud physically blocks sound by sitting in your ear - there's no active circuitry. No battery, no noise-cancelling chip. In practice, a proper in-ear seal cuts a meaningful amount of low-level ambient noise: the hum of a bus, background chatter, the drone of a fan. It won't touch loud. Sudden sounds, and it won't come close to what active cancellation does on a plane. If your goal is blocking out a noisy commute, passive isolation gets you part of the way there and no further. Sound The frequency range is 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. That's the standard full range of human hearing. And it's the number almost every earbud quotes regardless of how it actually sounds. It tells you the drivers are rated to reproduce the full audible spectrum; it doesn't tell you how balanced or detailed that reproduction is. For most people, the listing calls the audio quality "remarkable," which is marketing language, not a measurement. What you can reasonably infer from the rest of the spec - no amp, no battery, a simple wired connection - is that these are entry-level drivers doing a basic job. They'll play music, speech, and audio for video clearly enough. They aren't aimed at anyone judging soundstage or bass response. The connection and the cord The plug is a 3.5mm right-angle jack. Two things here. First. 3.5mm is the standard analog headphone jack, and the listing confirms compatibility with iPhones, iPads, Chromebooks, laptops, PCs, Android tablets, MP3 players, CD players, and fitness equipment. Read that compatibility list carefully, though: several recent iPhone models and many newer phones have dropped the 3.5mm jack entirely, so "compatible with iPhone" applies only to models that still have the port or to devices using an adapter. If your phone has no headphone jack. You'll need a dongle, and the listing doesn't include one. Second. The right-angle (L-shaped) plug is the practical choice for this kind of product. It sits flush against the device instead of sticking straight out. Which reduces leverage on the jack and resists accidental pull-outs - useful when a laptop gets shifted on a desk or a phone gets shoved into a pocket while still plugged in. The cord is listed at 4 feet. That's shorter than the 6-foot cables common on over-ear headphones but longer than the stubby cords on many entry-level earbuds, and it's a reasonable length for a phone in a pocket, a laptop on a desk, or a tablet held in your hands. The listing calls the cord tangle-free. Treat that with some skepticism - flat or specially coated cables reduce tangling but rarely eliminate it, and there's no flat-cable or braided spec here to back the claim up. Note also that the spec sheet lists "Retractable" under cable features, but nothing in the description or features describes a retractable mechanism, and a retractable cord on a 100-pack of disposable earbuds would be unusual. That line looks like a data error; don't count on a retracting reel. Water resistance The spec is explicit: not water resistant.
