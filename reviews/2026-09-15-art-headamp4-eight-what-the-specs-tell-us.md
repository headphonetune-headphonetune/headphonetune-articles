# ART HeadAMP4 Eight: What the Specs Tell Us

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/art-headamp4-eight-what-the-specs-tell-us/)

Four people crowded around a mixing desk. Each needing their own headphone feed with separate volume, is the exact scenario this box was built for. The ART HeadAmp4 is a distribution amplifier: one stereo source in, four independent stereo outputs out - and enough jacks to hang eight pairs of headphones off it if you double up. That's the whole pitch. And the specs back it up without much decoration. Output channels and what four really buys you The headline number is four output channels, each with its own volume knob. That's four separate stereo mixes, not four copies of one fixed level. For a home or project studio. That splits cleanly: a vocalist who wants it loud, a guitarist who wants it quiet, an engineer who needs reference level, and a spare feed for whoever wanders in. Each person adjusts their own knob without touching anyone else's. The "8 output" language in the attribute list is worth untangling, because it's not eight independent channels. Each of the four channels carries two headphone jacks - one 1/4-inch and one 1/8-inch - and they run simultaneously. So eight pairs of headphones can be plugged in at once, but they share a channel's volume control in pairs. Two people on channel one get the same level. That's fine for a pair of bandmates who agree on volume, less fine if one wants whisper-quiet and the other wants it cranked. If you genuinely need eight people at eight different levels, this isn't that product. Four independent zones is the real ceiling. Connectors: the 1/4 and 1/8 question Both jack sizes appear on the inputs and the outputs. The inputs accept either a 1/4-inch or 1/8-inch stereo source. So you can feed it from a mixer's main outs, an audio interface, a phone, a laptop headphone jack, or a keyboard - no adapter hunting. Compatible devices are listed as anything with a line-level source or a headphone output, which is deliberately broad and accurate. A headphone output feeding another amplifier isn't ideal from a noise standpoint, but it works, and that flexibility matters when you're patching together whatever gear you already own. On the output side, the mix of sizes is genuinely useful. Most studio headphones ship with 1/4-inch plugs. While earbuds and consumer sets use 1/8-inch. Having both on every channel means nobody in the room needs a dongle. That sounds like a small thing until you're mid-session and someone's adapter has vanished. Power output and drive capability The rated output is 4 watts. And the amp is solid state. Four watts across a headphone amp is a healthy figure - it's not a spec that tells you loudness in absolute terms. Since that depends on headphone impedance and sensitivity, but it signals the amp has real current on tap rather than a weak op-amp stage. Practically, this matters most with higher-impedance studio cans, the 250-ohm and 600-ohm models that cheaper splitters struggle to drive. An amp that can push voltage into a high-impedance load will keep those headphones sounding full instead of thin and quiet. The spec sheet doesn't break the 4 watts down by impedance, so treat it as a general indication of headroom rather than a precise promise at every load. Output impedance is specified at 47 ohms per output. That's a moderate figure. It's not the near-zero output impedance of a dedicated hi-fi amp. And with very low-impedance in-ear monitors, 47 ohms can subtly shift the frequency response. For typical studio headphones in the 32-to-600-ohm range, it's a non-issue. If you plan to run sensitive multi-driver IEMs through this, be aware the impedance interaction might color the sound slightly. For everything else, it's fine. Noise and distortion The capability list claims lower noise, lower distortion, and more output than whatever it's replacing. That's comparative language with no numbers attached - no THD figure, no signal-to-noise ratio, no frequency response curve. So you can't verify the claim from the spec sheet. What you can say is that the design intent is a clean signal path, and the 294 user ratings average 4.4 out of 5, which is a solid if not great consensus. For comparison, reviews at that level usually mean the product does its job well with occasional complaints, not that it's flawless. The absence of measured noise figures is a real gap if you're comparing it against a dedicated monitoring amp at a higher price point. Input and output levels Maximum input and maximum output levels are listed as headings with no values underneath. That's an incomplete spec. In practice. It means you'll want to feed the HeadAmp4 a normal line-level signal and not a speaker-level output, which would damage it. Standard consumer and pro line levels are well within what a device like this handles, but without published numbers, there's no way to confirm headroom for unusually hot sources. If your interface outputs at +4 dBu, that's almost certainly fine; if you're pushing something exotic, you'd have to test it. Size. Weight, and build At 1.1 pounds, this is light enough to toss in a gig bag and solid enough to sit on a desk without sliding around. The enclosure is extruded metal, and the case is described as stackable - meaning the chassis shape is designed to sit on top of matching ART units without wobbling. That's a real consideration for anyone building a rack of petite outboard gear. Metal rather than plastic matters here because a headphone amp gets cables yanked in and out constantly; a metal chassis survives that abuse far better than a plastic shell, and it also helps shield the electronics from interference. At just over a pound. It won't feel like a brick, but the extruded construction suggests it's more solid than a molded-plastic alternative at the same size. The color listing is odd - black. Purple, and white are all named, which usually means the listing is aggregating variants or the data is muddled. Don't count on choosing a color; assume black unless the seller specifies otherwise. Power The unit runs on corded electric power via a 12V DC adapter, which is included. There's no battery option and no mention of USB power. That means it's a desk or studio fixture, not something you'd use on a plane or bus. For everyday listening, it needs a wall outlet, and it's tethered by the length of the supplied adapter's cable, which isn't specified. For a fixed installation, that's completely fine. For anything portable, it's a dealbreaker. Inputs and outputs in practice The signal path is simple: one stereo input, four stereo output channels, each with independent volume. The amp splits whatever comes in and distributes it. There's no per-channel EQ, no talkback, no mixing - just clean distribution and level control. If you were hoping to blend two sources or send different material to different listeners, this won't do it. Every channel hears the same source; only the volume differs. That's the correct design for its purpose. But worth being clear about so nobody expects routing flexibility that isn't there. What the specs don't tell you Several things a buyer would reasonably want to know are missing. There's no frequency response range published, so you can't judge how far the amp extends at the low and high ends. No total harmonic distortion figure, no signal-to-noise ratio, no crosstalk spec between channels. Crosstalk matters in a multi-channel amp - if one listener's loud mix bleeds into another's quiet one. That's a problem, and there's no number to check. There's also no detailed impedance-versus-power curve, so the 4-watt figure is a single point, not a full picture. And there's no mention of what happens with a single pair of headphones plugged in versus eight - whether output level drops as more loads are added. In a passive splitter, it would; in an active amp like this, it shouldn't, but the specs don't confirm it. These gaps don't mean the product is poor. They mean the spec sheet alone can't answer every question, and the 4.4-star average from 294 ratings is doing some of the work that measurements would otherwise do. Compatibility The compatibility list is refreshingly broad: any device with a line-level source or a headphone output. That covers mixers, audio interfaces, keyboards, drum machines, laptops, phones with a headphone jack, and portable players. The dual 1/4-inch and 1/8-inch inputs mean you won't need adapters for most sources. This is a piece of gear designed to fit into whatever chain you already have rather than demanding a specific setup. Build and the stackable detail The extruded metal case and stackable design point to a product meant to live permanently in a studio rather than get carried around. Stackability is a compact but genuine convenience if you own other ART gear with the same footprint. In real-world use, it also suggests the unit is designed to run cool and sit still, which is what you want from something that's always powered on. The practical trade-offs The main limitation is the shared volume between the two jacks on each channel. If your use case involves two people on one channel who want different levels. You'll end up wishing for a fifth knob. The second is the wall power - no portability. The third is the missing measurement data, which makes direct comparison against dedicated monitoring amps harder than it should be. None of these are fatal for the intended use. But each one shapes who should buy this. On the plus side. The attribute set is exactly what a small studio needs and nothing it doesn't. Four independent channels, both jack sizes everywhere, a metal case that survives abuse, and enough output to drive real studio headphones. The 4.4-star average across nearly 300 ratings suggests the fundamentals hold up in practice. Summary:
The ART HeadAmp4 is a four-channel stereo headphone distribution amp that gives four listeners independent volume control and up to eight headphone connections. With both 1/4-inch and 1/8-inch jacks on every input and output and a metal, stackable chassis powered by an included 12V adapter. Its strengths are straightforward: genuine channel independence. Broad source compatibility, dual jack sizes that eliminate adapter hassles, and enough output to drive high-impedance studio headphones. The trade-offs are equally clear - the two jacks on each channel share one volume knob, it needs wall power with no battery option, and the spec sheet omits noise, distortion, frequency response, and impedance-versus-power figures, so you're partly relying on the strong user rating rather than published measurements. Anyone who needs eight truly independent levels, portable operation, or verified low-noise performance should keep looking. It's also a satisfactory fit for anyone with a mix of headphone types, since the dual jacks mean studio cans and consumer earbuds both plug in directly. If you already own other stackable ART gear, the matching chassis is a miniature bonus. Who should look elsewhere Skip this if you need eight people at eight different volumes - the paired jacks on each channel cap you at four independent levels. Skip it if you need portability. Since it runs only on wall power. Skip it if you're driving sensitive in-ear monitors and care about the 47-ohm output impedance coloring the response, or if you need published noise and distortion figures to compare against a dedicated monitoring amp.
