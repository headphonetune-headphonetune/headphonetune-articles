# Matast Wireless Earbuds: What the Specs Tell Us

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/matast-wireless-earbuds-what-the-specs-tell-us/)

You're mid-run. Sweat running into your ears, and the last thing you want is to stop and fish a fallen earbud off the pavement. That's the exact scenario Matast is targeting with the C16, an in-ear sports model built around earhooks and a claimed 50-hour total battery. The data paints a fairly clear picture: this is a budget-oriented fitness headset that leans on secure fit, long battery life, and water resistance rather than premium audio engineering. The specs are detailed enough to form real judgments. But there are some gaps worth flagging before you commit. Let's start with the physical design, since that's the whole pitch here. These are in-ear buds with flexible earhooks that loop over the ear to lock the bud in place. If you've ever had standard buds pop out during a sprint or a heavy set, you know why that matters. The earhook design is the single most relevant feature for anyone doing high-impact activity, and Matast includes three sizes of ear caps to help dial in the seal. That said, the spec sheet doesn't list the weight of each bud, which is a real omission for a fitness product. Lighter buds stay put better and cause less fatigue over a long session. And without that number you're guessing. The enclosure material is listed as plastic. Which is standard at this tier and keeps things light, though it won't feel high-end in the hand. The fit system has a trade-off baked in. Earhooks are great for stability, but they can be fussy to put on and take off, and they sometimes interfere with glasses or helmets. If you wear sunglasses while running or a bike helmet on rides, the hook has to coexist with that arm or strap. That's not a dealbreaker, but it's the kind of friction that shows up in daily use and not in a spec table. The three ear cap sizes help with the seal, but seal quality also depends on your ear anatomy, which no spec can capture. Now the audio hardware. There's a 13mm dynamic driver, which is on the larger side for earbuds. Bigger drivers generally move more air, which tends to produce stronger bass response. For workout music, that's usually welcome, and Matast claims powerful bass with a mellow midrange and clear treble. Take the marketing language with a grain of salt. But the 13mm figure is a legitimate data point that suggests a bass-forward tuning rather than a neutral, analytical sound. The impedance is listed at 10 ohms, which is quite low. Low impedance means the buds are easy to drive, so your phone or laptop won't struggle to get them loud. That's a practical plus for wireless buds, though in a Bluetooth context the amp is built into the bud anyway, so impedance matters less than it would for wired gear. Still, it's a sign the drivers are well-designed. What's missing on the audio side is any codec information. The Bluetooth spec lists support for HFP, HSP, A2DP, and AVRCP, which are the standard profiles for calls and audio streaming, but there's no mention of AAC, [aptX](https://www.aptx.com/), or [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac)we noticed . That means you're almost certainly getting the baseline SBC codec, which is fine for casual listening and workouts but won't satisfy anyone chasing [high-resolution audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/)we could see our analysis shows . The product name says "Hifi Stereo," but the profile list doesn't back that up with anything beyond standard Bluetooth audio. If you mainly listen to podcasts, pop, and workout playlists, this is a non-issue. If you care about detail and soundstage. This isn't the pair for you. The Bluetooth version is 5.4, which is genuinely current. The data claims data transmission is twice as fast as 5.3 and power consumption is 30% lower. And while those comparisons are somewhat promotional, the underlying point holds: 5.4 is a recent standard with better efficiency and connection stability than older versions. The stated range is 15 meters, which is about 49 feet* (based on available data). In practice, that's plenty for a gym floor or a run where your phone is on an armband, though walls and bodies will cut that distance down. The wide device compatibility is real and useful; it'll pair with iOS. Android, tablets, and laptops. There's no headphone jack. So this is wireless-only, which is expected for the category. Battery life is where the C16 looks strongest on paper. Matast claims 10 hours of music per charge and 50 hours total with the charging case. Ten hours from buds this size is a solid figure. And 50 hours total means you could go a week or more of daily workouts without hunting for a charger. The case charges over [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)we found and reaches full in 1.5 hours, which is reasonable. The dual LED display showing both case and bud charge levels is a small but genuinely useful capability; guessing your remaining battery before a long run is annoying. And a visible readout solves that. One caveat: the 10-hour figure is almost certainly measured at moderate volume with the noise features off. Real-world numbers with calls and higher volume will be lower. The spec sheet also doesn't clarify whether the 1.5-hour charge time applies to the case alone or the case plus buds, so treat that as approximate. Call quality gets some attention here. The C16 uses ENC, or Environmental Noise Cancellation, with microphones that Matast says passively cancel 80% of background noise. That figure is doing a lot of work. And "80%" isn't a standardized measurement, so don't read it as a lab-verified number. What matters practically is that the ENC focuses on your voice for calls rather than blocking noise for your ears. This is A relevant distinction: these aren't active [noise cancelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headphones. There's no [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) here. The "[noise cancelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)" language in the listing refers to the call microphone system, not to silencing your environment. If you want to mute a plane cabin or a loud office, these won't do that. For gym or outdoor use. Where you often want to hear traffic or a training partner, the lack of [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) is a aspect rather than a flaw. Durability is a clear strength on paper. The IP7 rating means the buds are waterproof, not just splash-resistant. IP7 is rated for immersion in water up to a meter for 30 minutes, which is more protection than most fitness buds offer. In practice, that covers sweat, rain, and even a rinse under the tap after a workout. The nano-coating is on the buds themselves. And the data doesn't specify a rating for the charging case, so keep the case dry. For anyone who sweats heavily or trains outdoors in poor weather, the IP7 rating is one of the more compelling reasons to consider these. Controls are touch-based, and the feature list says you can play, pause, answer calls, skip tracks, and wake Siri without touching your phone. Touch controls are time-saving but notoriously finicky, especially with sweaty or gloved fingers, and accidental triggers during a run are common. The spec sheet doesn't detail how customizable the touch zones are, so there's no way to know if you can remap them. That's a gap worth noting. Pairing is automatic after the first installation: open the case and the buds connect. That's standard now, but it's a genuine convenience. Now the reputation data. There are 88 customer reviews with an average of 4.9 out of 5 stars. That's a very high average. Though the sample size is modest. A 4.9 from 88 buyers suggests strong early satisfaction, but it's not the same as thousands of ratings, and a handful of unhappy buyers can move a petite sample more than a large one. The best sellers rank is #318 in Earbud & In-Ear Headphones and #3,562 in Electronics All things considered, which indicates decent but not dominant sales volume. That ranking tells you these are a known quantity moving steadily. Not a viral hit or a clearance oddity. What's in the box is thin: the listing says only "User Manual." In practice you'd expect the charging case and ear caps to be included. Since both are described in the features, but the box contents section doesn't list them. That's likely an incomplete listing rather than a genuine omission, but I can't confirm from the data what actually ships. Worth checking before you buy if that matters to you. Missing information is worth cataloging. Because it affects how confidently you can judge this product. There's no weight per bud. There's no codec support listed beyond the fundamental profiles. There's no charging case capacity in mAh. There's no latency figure, which matters if you plan to use these for gaming or watching video where audio-video sync is critical. There's no multipoint pairing mentioned. So you likely can't connect to a phone and laptop simultaneously. And there's no detail on the touch control customization. None of these are fatal. But they're the questions a careful buyer would want answered before committing. On value, the feature set is dense for the category: [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/). IP7, 50-hour total battery, a dual LED display, earhooks, and ENC call mics. That's a lot of boxes ticked. The trade-offs are the ones you'd expect at this tier: no [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control), no premium codecs, plastic build - and touch controls that may or may not behave. If your priorities are staying put during a workout, surviving sweat and rain, and not charging constantly, the C16 lines up well. If your priorities are pristine audio, [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control), or a premium feel, look at higher tiers. The 2-year warranty is a meaningful detail that's easy to overlook. Two years is longer than many budget earbuds offer. And for a fitness product that gets sweat and impact, that coverage adds real peace of mind. For everyday listening. It suggests Matast is willing to stand behind the hardware, at least on paper. Just note the data doesn't specify what the warranty covers, so read the terms. Summary: The Matast C16 is a sports-focused pair of wireless earbuds that prioritizes secure fit, water resistance, and long battery life over high-end audio or noise cancellation. Its strongest data points are the IP7 waterproof rating, [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/), 50-hour total battery with a dual LED display, and earhooks with three ear cap sizes, backed by a 2-year warranty. The main compromises are the absence of [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). No listed quality codecs, a plastic build, and several missing specs including bud weight and latency. Buyers who want [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control), [high-resolution audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/). Or detailed fit metrics should look at other options. Who this is for This is for someone who trains hard and wants buds that stay in. Runners. Gym-goers, cyclists - and anyone who sweats heavily will appreciate the earhook design and IP7 rating, which together address the two most common failure points for workout earbuds. It also suits people who hate charging frequently; 10 hours per charge and 50 hours total means you can leave the case at home for a long session or go days between charges. If you take a lot of calls while moving and want your voice to come through clearly, the ENC mic system is aimed at you. And if you want a recent Bluetooth standard for stable connections without paying a superior. The 5.4 chip is a legitimate draw. Who should look elsewhere Skip these if noise cancellation is a priority. There's no [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control), so they won't quiet a noisy commute, an open office, or a flight. Look elsewhere if you care about audio codecs or high-resolution playback; the profile list points to standard Bluetooth audio, not [aptX](https://www.aptx.com/) or [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac). If you wear glasses or a helmet regularly, the earhooks may clash with those, so consider a hookless design. If you're sensitive to touch controls or want remappable buttons, the finicky nature of touch and the lack of customization detail make these a risk. And if you need low latency for gaming or video editing, the absence of a latency spec means you can't count on these for sync-significant work. Finally. If you want a top-tier metal build or in-depth fit data like bud weight, the spec sheet doesn't deliver it. **💡 Key Takeaway:** Our analysis of the Matast Wireless Earbuds is based entirely on published specifications and available product information.
