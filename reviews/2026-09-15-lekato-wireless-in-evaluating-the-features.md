A worship leader mid-set, a guitarist tracking overdubs in a home studio, a singer rehearsing with a band - these are the people a wireless in-ear monitor system is built for. The LEKATO MS-02 is an entry-level take on that category - and the most important thing to know before anything else is right in the listing: earbuds are not included. You supply your own. That single omission shapes the entire value calculation. So keep it in mind as you read the rest. The MS-02 is a two-piece kit: a transmitter and a receiver. The transmitter takes an audio signal from your source (a mixer. Interface. Phone, laptop, whatever) via the included 3.5mm cable or through the 3.5mm-to-6.35mm adapter, and sends it wirelessly over 2.4GHz to the beltpack receiver, which clips to your clothing and plugs into your earbuds. That's the whole system. It replaces the long cable you'd otherwise run from the mixer to your ears. Latency The headline number is 4.5 milliseconds, and LEKATO is specific about how that's measured: it's the figure in mono mode. Stereo mode is likely to be somewhat higher, though the listing doesn't say how much. For context on what 4.5ms means in practice. That's roughly the time it takes sound to travel about 5 feet through air. Musicians generally start noticing latency as a problem somewhere around 10ms or more, where it makes timing feel disconnected, especially for vocals and drums. At under 4.5ms. This is in territory most players would describe as usable without fighting it. Compared to similar 2.4GHz IEM systems in this class, that's a competitive figure - plenty of budget wireless systems sit closer to 6-8ms. It's not the lowest on the market, but it's comfortably in the range where it shouldn't get in your way. Worth noting: the listing says "Mono mode <4.5ms" but doesn't give a stereo figure. If you need stereo and low latency simultaneously, that's an unknown. Audio quality The sampling rate is 24-bit/48kHz. That's a standard, honestly modest figure for digital audio - 48kHz is the same rate used for most video production and live sound work, and 24-bit is the common bit depth. It's not [high-resolution audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/)we noticed by any stretch. But for monitoring purposes (hearing yourself and the mix, not mastering), it's fine. The category standard at this price is similar. The audio driver type is listed as "balanced armature." Here's the drawback: balanced armature describes a type of driver used inside earbuds. And the listing explicitly states earbuds aren't included. So either the spec is describing the earbuds you'd need to buy separately (which LEKATO doesn't sell as part of this kit). Or it's a data error. The system itself is a transmitter and receiver - it doesn't have a driver. Treat that spec line with skepticism. What you actually hear will depend entirely on the earbuds you plug in. The frequency range is given as 20Hz-24kHz. That's a wide range on paper, covering the full audible spectrum and a bit above. But again. This figure is more meaningful for a headphone driver than for a wireless transmission system. Realistically. The transmission will pass along whatever your source and your earbuds can handle. Don't read much into it. One spec that does matter: noise control is listed as "None." Battery life and charging LEKATO claims 6-8 hours of continuous operation on a full charge. The "battery average life" field says 7 hours; the "battery charge time" field says 6 hours. That charge time is on the long side - if you're doing a full day of rehearsals, six hours to refill means you can't just top it up quickly during a lunch break. Compare that to similar products: many wireless IEM systems in this class charge in 2-4 hours. Six hours is below average for the category. Even if the runtime itself is acceptable. The 6-8 hour runtime is reasonable for a single gig or a rehearsal, and it's longer than the MS-01 it replaces, which is a genuine improvement. But if you run long sessions back to back, plan around the slow recharge. The receiver has a 5-level volume adjustment with LED indicators showing battery level and volume. Five steps is coarse - you may find yourself wanting something between steps 3 and 4. Compare that to systems with continuous or finer volume control, and this is a limitation. For casual use it's fine; for essential monitoring where you're dialing in a precise level, it'll feel restrictive. Range The listed range is 100 feet (about 30 meters) in open air. The listing also gives a Bluetooth range of 30.48 meters - which is just the metric equivalent of 100 feet. And slightly odd because this isn't a Bluetooth device; it's 2.4GHz RF. Don't read anything into the Bluetooth field. The 100-foot figure is the one to use. 100 feet in open air is typical for 2.4GHz systems at this price. What matters more is what happens indoors. 2.4GHz is the same band used by Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and plenty of other wireless gear. Walls. Bodies, and other transmitters will cut that range markedly. In a typical venue or studio. Expect usable range closer to 30-50 feet* (based on available data). Sometimes less if there's interference. One transmitter to multiple receivers The system compatible with one transmitter sending to up to four receivers simultaneously. This is genuinely useful for a band or a small worship team where multiple people need the same monitor mix. The category standard for budget systems is often one-to-one or one-to-two; four is above average for this class. The pairing process is manual: turn on the transmitter and a receiver. Hold both pairing buttons until they blink, wait for solid lights. Repeat for each additional receiver. Not involved, but not automatic either, despite the "auto-pairing" in the title. Once paired, the connection should hold. If you need each performer to hear a different mix, this system won't do that - it sends one signal to everyone. For a shared monitor mix, it's fine.
