# Nerunsa 2026 Wireless: Features &amp; Specs Guide

You're on a packed train, someone two seats over is on a speakerphone call, and you want to disappear into a podcast. That's the scenario these earbuds are built for. And the specs tell a fairly specific story about how well they'd handle it. The Nerunsa D60 is a budget wireless earbud with a few genuinely useful features and a few places where the marketing numbers deserve a second look. Three things matter most here: the [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) radio, the battery setup, and the ENC microphone array. Those are the specs that will actually shape your daily experience. The rest - driver size, waterproofing, weight - fills in the picture but won't make or break the purchase. [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) and what the version number actually buys you The D60 runs [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) with dual-channel transmission. And it supports the standard protocol stack: HFP, A2DP, HSP, and AVRCP. In plain terms, that means it handles hands-free calls, stereo audio streaming, headset functions, and remote control commands like play/pause and track skip. Nothing exotic, but nothing missing either. The claimed improvements over [Bluetooth 5.3](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) - 20% faster connection, 25% better stability, 10% more interference resistance, 20% lower power draw, 30ms less latency - are manufacturer figures, so treat them as directional rather than gospel. What they translate to in practice is this: [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) is current-gen, and the dual-channel transmission matters more than the version number. Dual-channel means audio streams independently to each earbud rather than bouncing from one bud to the other. For everyday listening. The practical benefit is fewer dropouts when you turn your head or put your phone in a back pocket. If you've used older budget earbuds where the left bud cuts out when you walk past a microwave. That's the problem dual-channel transmission solves. The trade-off is that the [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) only helps if your phone compatible with it. Most phones from the last two or three years do. But an older device will fall back to whatever it can negotiate. You'll still get a connection. Just not the full benefit. Latency is the other consideration. Even with a 30ms reduction, Bluetooth audio isn't wired audio. For music and podcasts, nobody will notice. For competitive gaming or rhythm games where split-second timing matters, you'll still feel a slight delay. For video, most phones compensate automatically, so lip-sync should be fine. One thing worth flagging: there's no mention of codec support beyond the standard protocols. No [aptX](https://www.aptx.com/), no [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac), no AAC listed. That means you're getting SBC, the baseline Bluetooth codec, or whatever the earbuds negotiate by default. For casual listening this is fine. For anyone hoping to squeeze [high-resolution audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/) out of a streaming service, the wireless link itself will be the bottleneck, not the drivers. That's a real limitation at this price point, though an expected one. Battery life: the 50-hour claim and what it really means The headline number is 50 hours of total playtime, but the breakdown matters more. Each earbud delivers up to 8 hours of continuous playback on a single charge. In real-world use. The charging case extends that to 50 hours total. So the 50-hour figure assumes you're cycling the buds back into the case between sessions, which is how everyone actually uses these things. Eight hours per charge is solid for true wireless earbuds - enough for a full workday, a long flight, or several gym sessions before you need the case. Charging happens over [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c). And a full charge takes about 1.5 hours. That's average. Nothing speedy-charge about it. So if you're the type who realizes your earbuds are dead five minutes before leaving the house, plan ahead. There's no mention of a quick-charge capability that gives you A hour of playback from a few minutes in the case, which some competitors offer. That's a small but real convenience gap. The dual LED digital display on the case is a genuinely useful addition. It shows remaining battery for both the earbuds and the case separately. So you're not guessing whether the case itself requires charging. On cheaper earbuds you often get a single blinking light that tells you almost nothing. Having actual numbers is the kind of detail that matters more day-to-day than a marginally better driver. For most people. The trade-off with any battery claim is that the 8 hours is measured at moderate volume with the features you're actually using. Crank the volume, take a lot of calls, and you'll see less. That's true of every earbud, not just this one. The 50-hour total is also a best-case figure that assumes ideal conditions. Real-world use will land somewhat below that. But probably not dramatically so. The 13mm driver, ENC mics, and the sound profile The D60 uses a 13mm dynamic driver with a graphene-TPU composite diaphragm. On the practical side. The claimed result is richer mids and highs plus 60% stronger bass, with a 50% larger sound-producing area than the previous generation. Graphene-TPU is a composite material that aims to balance stiffness and flexibility, which in theory gives you cleaner highs without sacrificing low-end punch. Whether it delivers is something only your ears can judge. But the driver size is on the larger end for in-ear buds, and larger drivers generally move more air, which tends to mean better bass response. The "60% stronger bass" claim is a red flag for anyone who prefers neutral, accurate sound. Bass-heavy tuning is common in budget earbuds because it's an easy way to make inexpensive drivers sound impressive in a quick demo. If you listen to a lot of acoustic music. Classical, or anything where you want to hear the mix as the engineer intended, heavily boosted bass can muddy the mids and make everything sound thick. If you listen to pop. Hip-hop, EDM, or podcasts, the extra low-end will probably feel like a aspect rather than a flaw. Worth knowing which camp you're in before buying. The ENC (Environmental Noise Cancellation) configuration uses four microphones and claims to filter out 80% of background noise on calls. This is key to understand correctly: ENC isn't [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) actively blocks sound from reaching your ears so you hear less of the world around you. ENC only affects your voice on calls - it's the person on the other end who benefits, not you. You'll still hear the train, the gym, the office. The mics work to isolate your voice from that noise so your caller doesn't have to listen to it. That distinction matters because the product name and features list lead with "[noise cancelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). " which could easily be misread. If your goal is to block out a noisy commute so you can focus on music, these earbuds won't do that any better than a decent pair of silicone tips sealing your ear canal. If your goal is to take calls from a coffee shop without your caller complaining about the espresso machine, the four-mic array is the relevant spec. On that front. Four mics is a generous count for this price range, and ENC has gotten genuinely solid in recent years. Calls should sound clear. Though the 80% figure is a lab number, not a promise. Fit. Weight, and the IP7 rating The D60 weighs 3.9 grams per earbud, which is light. For reference, that's roughly the weight of a single sheet of paper folded a few times. You won't feel them after a few minutes. And for long wear sessions that matters. The ergonomic shape is designed to distribute ear pressure evenly. And three ear tip sizes are included. Getting a proper seal is the single most important factor in how any in-ear earbud sounds - a poor seal kills bass and lets outside noise in. Three sizes gives you a decent shot at a solid fit, though people with unusual ear shapes sometimes need a fourth or a specialty tip. The IP7 waterproof rating is more generous than the [IPX4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) or [IPX5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) you often see at this price. IP7 means the earbuds can survive being submerged in water up to a meter for 30 minutes. In practice. That covers heavy sweat, rain, and the occasional accidental drop in a puddle or sink. It doesn't mean you should swim with them. And the charging case is not rated, so don't dunk that. For gym use. Running in the rain, or just sweating heavily during a workout, IP7 is more protection than most people will ever need. For comparison. The trade-off with any in-ear design is comfort versus isolation. A tight seal blocks noise but can feel uncomfortable after a couple hours. A loose seal is easy to wear but lets everything in and sounds thin. The 3.9g weight helps here because lighter buds put less pressure on the ear canal, but fit is personal. There's no wing or hook design mentioned, so if you have ears that reject standard earbud shapes during vigorous exercise, these might work loose. That's a "try them and see" situation rather than something the specs can settle. Touch controls, pairing, and what's missing from the data Controls are touch-based: tap to play, pause, skip, adjust volume, and take calls. Touch controls on budget earbuds are hit-or-miss. They can be overly sensitive to accidental brushes or require a precise tap that's hard to land mid-run. The manual covers the specifics, and there's no way to know from the spec sheet how well the sensors are tuned. That's a genuine gap in the data. Pairing is straightforward: open the case. Take out the buds, and they auto-pair. That's standard behavior now and it works. Compatibility covers Android, iOS, laptops, and tablets over Bluetooth. What's not in the data: the exact Bluetooth range. Whether there's a low-latency gaming mode you can toggle manually, whether the earbuds support multipoint pairing (connecting to two devices at once), and what the touch controls can and can't do without the manual. If multipoint matters to you - say, staying connected to a laptop and phone simultaneously - that's not confirmed here, and it's a feature many budget earbuds skip. Assume single-device pairing unless you confirm otherwise. The warranty is listed as one year, which is standard. In daily use, the box includes a charging cable. No mention of a wall adapter, which is normal. Build and materials The enclosure is plastic. Color is Midnight Black. Plastic is the norm at this price point and keeps the weight down, but it won't feel superior. Nobody buys budget earbuds for the hand feel of the case. Though. And plastic holds up fine if you don't abuse it. The 4.4-star rating across 119 reviews suggests most buyers are satisfied. Though that's a modest sample size and skews toward early adopters. A product with this few reviews hasn't been through the long-term durability test that thousands of users over a year would provide. That's not a knock against these specific earbuds - it's just the reality of buying something with a short track record. Summary: The Nerunsa D60 covers the essentials well: [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) with dual-channel transmission. 8 hours per charge with a 50-hour total via the case, a four-mic ENC array for calls, IP7 waterproofing - and a light 3.9g fit. The main trade-offs are bass-forward tuning that may not suit neutral-sound listeners. ENC rather than true [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). No advanced codecs - and no confirmed multipoint pairing. It's a sensible pick if you want consistent wireless earbuds for workouts, commutes, and calls without spending much, and a poor fit if you need [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control), audiophile-grade sound, or multi-device connectivity. If you need to stay connected to a laptop and phone at the same time, confirm multipoint pairing before buying, because it's not in the specs.
