# JXMOX USB C: Top Features Compared (2026)

The JXMOX [USB C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)we noticed to 3.5mm aux cable is a passive adapter cable, not a headphone. It has no drivers, no battery, and no amplifier of its own. What it does is take the digital audio output from a [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) port and convert it to an analog signal that standard wired headphones. Car stereos, and speakers can accept. If you have a phone or tablet that dropped its headphone jack. This is the bridge back to wired listening. That's the entire pitch. Size, weight, and build The cable is 4 feet long and weighs 18.14 grams. Four feet is a practical length for most desk setups. Car dash runs, or walking around with a phone in a pocket. It's long enough to reach from a phone in a cupholder to a stereo input without a tangle of slack, and short enough that it won't snag on everything between your seat and the dashboard. Eighteen grams is light enough that you'll forget it's in your bag. Which matters if you keep one stashed in a laptop case or glovebox. The outer material is nylon braided in a grey finish. That's a meaningful upgrade over the rubber-jacketed cables that tend to split at the connector after a year of flexing in a pocket. JXMOX claims the strain relief design survives more than 12,000 bending tests. Treat that number with some skepticism, since it's a manufacturer figure and the testing method isn't specified. What it does suggest is that the connector joints are reinforced, which is where budget aux cables usually fail. The cable is round in cross-section, which helps it coil neatly and reduces the flat-cable tendency to kink at sharp angles. Sound quality and the DAC chip The cable uses a built-in DAC smart chip. Here's why that matters. [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) ports output digital audio, but a 3.5mm jack demands an analog signal. For everyday listening. The DAC is the component that converts one to the other. Phones and laptops used to have this chip built in alongside the headphone jack. Once the jack disappeared. The conversion had to move somewhere - either into the headphones themselves or into a dongle like this one. JXMOX describes the result as hi-fi, with noise reduction and higher fidelity. That's marketing language, and the spec sheet doesn't name the DAC model or list supported sample rates or bit depths. Without those details, there's no way to verify how the chip performs against a known reference. What can be said is that the DAC exists and handles the conversion. Which is the minimum required for the cable to function at all. Whether it delivers audibly better sound than a competing dongle is not something the specs can answer. For most listeners using this in a car or with a pair of everyday headphones, the difference between DAC chips is subtle at best, and often inaudible. One relevant figure: the data transfer rate is listed at 6 gigabits per second. That's a USB spec. Not an audio spec, and it's almost certainly the cable's maximum USB throughput rather than anything related to audio quality. Audio doesn't need anywhere near that bandwidth. Treat it as a note that the cable meets a certain USB standard. Not as evidence of superior sound. Power and electrical specs The maximum voltage is 2 volts of direct current, and the connector has 24 pins. The 2V ceiling is worth flagging. Standard [USB power delivery](https://www.usb.org/usb-charger-pd) runs at 5V and up, and this cable isn't designed to pass charging current. The low voltage rating reinforces what the description states plainly: this cable does not support charging. It's an audio-only path. That's a real limitation. If you're on a long drive and your phone is low, you can't charge and listen through this cable at the same time. You'd need a separate charging cable or a splitter, and even then the phone's [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) port can only do one thing at a time unless you have a powered hub or a phone with wireless charging. For a commuter running a 30-minute trip, that's fine. For a road trip where the phone is also the navigation device, it's a genuine inconvenience worth planning around. The [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) end plugs into your phone, tablet, or laptop, and the 3.5mm end accepts whatever you plug into it - headphones, an aux cable to a car stereo, a speaker input. For hands-free driving, that's a limitation - you'd need to hold the phone or rely on speakerphone, which defeats some of the purpose of a wired connection. The cable doesn't support wire control.
