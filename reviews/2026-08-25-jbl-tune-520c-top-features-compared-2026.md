You're sitting at your desk, laptop open - and you need a pair of headphones that just work. No pairing, no battery anxiety, no Bluetooth codec roulette. You want to plug in and hear your music clearly. But there's a catch the spec sheet hints at with a footnote: "Please check [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) compatibility with your device." Not every [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) port outputs analog audio. And some devices - particularly certain phones - may route audio differently or not at all. You're betting on your device playing nice. For most modern laptops and Android phones, you're fine. For older hardware or some niche devices, you might plug in and hear nothing. That's a real risk worth acknowledging before you buy. The upside of that [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) connection is that it enables the headline feature: [Hi-Res audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/) at 96kHz/24bit, handled by a built-in DSP chipset. What does that mean practically? If you're listening to [high-resolution audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/) files from a service like Tidal or Qobuz, or you have a local library of FLAC files, these headphones can actually resolve that extra detail. In real-world use, the DSP chip is doing the digital-to-analog conversion inside the headphone itself, which is a clever way to bypass the sometimes mediocre DACs in budget laptops and phones. You're getting a cleaner signal path than you would with a cheap dongle or a standard headphone jack. For a casual listener streaming Spotify at 320kbps, that Hi-Res badge won't change much - the source is the bottleneck. But if you care about audio fidelity and have the files to back it up, this is a meaningful aspect at this price point. Under the hood. You've got 32mm dynamic drivers with JBL's Pure Bass tuning. That's a familiar formula from JBL's consumer lineup. For most people, the frequency response is listed as 20Hz to 20kHz, which is the standard audible range and tells you nothing surprising - every headphone claims this. The sensitivity is 103dB, which is decently well-designed, and impedance is 32 ohms. What that means in real terms is that these will get loud enough from any phone or laptop without needing a separate amplifier. You won't be cranking a volume knob to max and wishing for more. The 32mm driver size is on the smaller side for on-ear headphones. But it's typical for this category and fine for portability. The bass emphasis is the JBL house sound - it's boosted, punchy, and designed to make pop, hip-hop, and electronic music feel energetic. On the practical side. It's not neutral, and it's not audiophile-flat. If you want clinical accuracy, look elsewhere. If you want music to feel fun and lively, this tuning works. You get three EQ presets controlled by a button on the remote: Default, Bass, and Vocal. That's a small but useful characteristic. The Default setting is the well-rounded one for everyday listening. Bass boosts the low end further for when you want extra thump. Vocal lifts the midrange, which helps with podcasts, dialogue-heavy content, or music where you want clearer vocals. Having these on the remote rather than in an app is actually a plus - you can switch without pulling out your phone. It's a simple, tactile workaround that works on any device, regardless of whether you have the JBL app installed. The remote itself is a three-button inline control with a microphone. Play/pause, volume up and down, and call answering are all there. For comparison. The mic is positioned on the cable, which is fine for calls but not great - you'll want to be reasonably close to the mic for clear speech. It's fine for a quick call in a quiet room or a Zoom meeting at your desk. For noisy environments, the lack of any noise cancellation on the mic means the person on the other end will hear background noise. The data lists "Calling" and "Office Meetings" as intended uses. And that's accurate as long as you set expectations: this is a simple call mic, not a broadcast-quality setup. Speaking of noise - there's no [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) here. The spec sheet lists "Passive Noise Cancellation," which is marketing speak for "the ear cups block some sound by virtue of being on your head." On-ear headphones like this don't seal around your ear; they sit on top of it. That means they leak sound in and out more than over-ear models. In a quiet office, they're fine. On a noisy commute or an airplane, you'll hear a lot of ambient noise bleeding through. The 32mm drivers and on-ear design also mean you'll feel some pressure on your ears after a couple of hours. Comfort is subjective, but on-ear headphones generally get less comfortable for long listening sessions than over-ears. At 150 grams, these are very light - you won't feel the weight on your head - but the clamp force and ear pad pressure are the limiting factors. In daily use. The foldable design is a nice touch for tossing them in a bag, and the flat, tangle-free cable is genuinely practical. The cable connection is reinforced at the headphone end, which addresses the most common failure point on wired headphones. Build quality is what you'd expect for the price: plastic construction.
