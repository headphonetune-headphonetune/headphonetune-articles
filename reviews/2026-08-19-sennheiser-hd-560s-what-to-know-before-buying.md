# Sennheiser HD 560S: What to Know Before Buying

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/sennheiser-hd-560s-what-to-know-before-buying/)

The Sennheiser HD 560S is a wired, open-back headphone aimed at people who care about accurate sound more than convenience. If you're sitting at a desk, at home, and want to hear exactly what is in your audio files, this is a serious contender. If you want something for the bus, or something that blocks out the office chatter, this isn't it. Let's walk through what the specs actually mean for you. Build & Materials The HD 560S weighs 240 grams. That is light. Many over-ear headphones push 300 grams* (based on available data) or more, and you feel that difference after a couple of hours. The ear pads are velour, which is a breathable fabric that doesn't trap heat the way leather or synthetic leather does. The earcups are described as "ventilated," which is part of the open-back design. This isn't a luxury metal build; it's a practical plastic construction that keeps weight down. The trade-off is that it does not feel as premium as a metal headphone, but it is long-lasting enough for home use. If you're rough with gear, or plan to toss these in a bag, the plastic hinges and yokes are the weak points to watch. For everyday listening, they aren't fragile, but they aren't built like a tank either. The headphone does not fold. The listing says "Over Ear" under folding features, which means it is a fixed shape. that's a consideration if you travel; these take up space in a bag and don't collapse into a compact form. For desk use, that's irrelevant. The Open-Back Design: The Biggest Trade-Off This is the defining characteristic. Open-back headphones have perforated earcups that let air pass through. The Sennheiser HD 560S uses this design to create a wide soundstage, which is the sense of space and direction in the audio. Sound doesn't feel like it's trapped inside your head; it feels like it's coming from around you. For gaming, this means you can pinpoint footsteps or directional cues more accurately than with most closed-back headphones. For music, it means instruments are separated and placed in a virtual space rather than mashed together. The cost is isolation. In real-world use, these leak sound out. Someone sitting next to you will hear what you're listening to, especially at moderate volumes. They also let sound in. You will hear the hum of a refrigerator, the clack of a keyboard, the murmur of a conversation. This isn't a headphone for a shared office or a noisy room. It is for a quiet space where you can sit still and listen. Performance Expectations The frequency range is listed as 6 Hz to 38 kHz. The human ear hears roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz, so this extends beyond audible limits on both ends. That spec is more about headroom than anything you will directly perceive; it indicates the drivers are capable of reproducing the full audible spectrum without strain. The key part is the tuning, which Sennheiser describes as neutral and accurate. That means the bass isn't boosted, the mids aren't recessed, and the treble isn't artificially sharpened. What you get is a flat, honest representation of the recording. For most people, the impedance is 120 ohms. This is higher than typical consumer headphones, which usually sit around 32 ohms. High impedance means the headphone calls for more voltage to reach the same volume. Plugging these directly into a laptop or phone will work, but the volume may be lower than you expect, and the sound may lack punch. The sensitivity is 110 dB, which is fairly well-designed, so it isn't extremely hard to drive. In practice, a decent laptop headphone jack will get you to a reasonable listening level, but a dedicated headphone amplifier or a DAC with a built-in amp will make these sound noticeably better. On the practical side, the listing explicitly mentions compatibility with PCs, laptops, DACs, and home stereos, which signals the intended use case: stationary setups, not portable devices. The driver is dynamic, which is the most common type. It uses a moving coil and magnet to produce sound. This isn't exotic technology, but it's well-understood and dependable. The angled driver alignment, which Sennheiser calls E.A.R., tilts the drivers to mimic the position of speakers in a room. that's a real aspect, not marketing fluff; it contributes to the sense of depth and imaging. Sound Signature in Practice Because the tuning is neutral, these headphones will not flatter subpar recordings. If a track is compressed or poorly mixed, you will hear it. If a game has muddy audio, you will hear that too. That is the point of a reference headphone. For music lovers, this means you hear the artist's intent rather than a colored version of it. Bass is present but not thumping; if you want booming, chest-vibrating low end, these aren't the headphones for you. For comparison, the treble is detailed and can be slightly forward, which helps with clarity but may be fatiguing for listeners sensitive to high frequencies over long sessions. For gaming, the open-back design and neutral tuning work well. Directional audio is precise because the soundstage is wide and the imaging is accurate. You can tell if footsteps are above, below, or to the side. The lack of bass emphasis means explosions don't have the visceral impact you get from gaming headsets, but that's a trade-off for clarity. If you play competitive shooters, these are great. If you play single-player games and want cinematic rumble, look elsewhere. Comfort and Fit The velour pads and 240-gram weight are the headline comfort specs. Velour is soft and breathable, which helps during long listening sessions. The ventilated earcups reduce heat buildup, so your ears don't get sweaty after A hour. The clamping force isn't listed, but based on the design philosophy of the HD 5 series, it's moderate. The headphones are designed to be "barely there," per the description, which suggests a light touch rather than a vice grip. For glasses wearers, velour is generally more forgiving than leather because it compresses less and doesn't grip as hard. The earcups are over-ear, meaning they surround the ear rather than sitting on it, which is better for extended wear. There is a catch. Open-back headphones let ambient noise in, so if your environment is noisy, you will find yourself turning the volume up to compensate, which defeats the purpose of a neutral listening experience. In a quiet room, these are user-friendly for hours. In a cafe, you will be distracted. What Is Included The box contains the headphones, a detachable cable, a 6.35mm to 3.5mm adapter, and a quick-start guide. The cable terminates in a 6.35mm plug, which is the larger quarter-inch connector used on home audio equipment and amplifiers. The adapter lets you use it with the standard 3.5mm jack on laptops, phones, and gaming controllers. The cable is detachable, which is decent for longevity; if the cable breaks, you can replace it without sending the whole headphone in. The cable isn't balanced, and there's no microphone. If you want to chat in games or take calls, you need a separate mic. Connectivity and Compatibility This is a wired headphone. there's no Bluetooth, no battery, no noise cancellation, no touch controls. The listing mentions "control method: Touch," but that appears to be a data error; there are no controls on the cable or the earcups. You plug it in and it plays. that's the entire feature set. The compatibility list includes desktops, laptops, record players, and home audio systems. In daily use, it will work with a phone via the adapter, but the 120-ohm impedance means a phone may not drive it to satisfying volume, especially with a dongle that lacks a dedicated amp. The water resistance is listed as "Not Water Resistant." don't wear these in the rain, don't sweat on them heavily, and don't leave them near a drink. They are for stationary indoor use. What isn't Clear from the Data The listing doesn't specify the cable length. That matters for desk setups; a short cable limits where you can place your amplifier or PC. it's likely around 3 meters based on typical Sennheiser designs, but that isn't confirmed in the data. For typical buyers, the build quality is described as "sturdy materials," but there is no specific information about the headband padding or the hinge mechanism. Sennheiser has a strong reputation, and the customer rating of 4.6 out of 5 stars from over 3,700 reviews suggests consistent satisfaction, but the data doesn't tell you how these hold up after two years of daily use. The clamping force isn't listed. This is a personal fit factor; some people find Sennheiser's open-back models comfortable, others find them too loose or too tight. The velour pads and light weight mitigate this, but without trying them on, you're guessing. Value Considerations The HD 560S sits in a specific spot. For everyday listening, it isn't a budget headphone, but it isn't a flagship either. It is priced for someone who wants accurate sound without spending thousands. The neutral tuning and open-back design are features you usually have to pay more for. The 120-ohm impedance is a hurdle; if you don't have an amplifier, you aren't getting the full performance. that's an additional cost to factor in if you're starting from scratch. The lack of a microphone is a real limitation for gamers. Most gaming headsets include a mic, and if you do not have a separate installation, you will need to buy one. That adds to the total cost and clutter. The open-back design also means you need a quiet room, which is not a given for everyone. The customer reviews are strong, which indicates that most buyers are satisfied with what they got. The "Low Returns" note from the brand data suggests people aren't sending these back in droves. that's a decent sign, but it doesn't tell you about long-term reliability. Who this is for This is for the person who sits at a desk, in a quiet room, and wants to hear their music or games accurately. It is for the audio enthusiast who is building a home assembly with a DAC or amplifier and wants a reference point. it's for the competitive gamer who values positional audio over bass rumble. it's for the content creator who demands to hear the details in a mix to make editing decisions. it's for someone who prioritizes sound quality over convenience and doesn't mind a wire. Who should look elsewhere If you need noise isolation, these are wrong. On a plane, on a train, in an office, these will let in everything and leak out everything. If you want bass-heavy sound for hip-hop or action movies, the neutral tuning will disappoint. If you do not have an amplifier or a decent sound card, you will not get the full benefit, and a lower-impedance headphone might serve you better. If you need a microphone for calls or gaming, these don't have one. If you want wireless freedom, Bluetooth, or noise cancellation, these aren't for you. If you listen in a shared space and don't want to annoy others with your audio, the open-back design is a dealbreaker. Summary: The Sennheiser HD 560S is a wired, open-back headphone built for accurate sound reproduction in a quiet, stationary environment. It offers a wide soundstage, neutral tuning, and light, breathable comfort, making it a strong choice for notable listening and competitive gaming. The 120-ohm impedance means you will likely need an amplifier to get the best performance, and the lack of a microphone and noise isolation limits its versatility. If you want honest, detailed audio and have a quiet space with a decent source, this is a solid investment.
