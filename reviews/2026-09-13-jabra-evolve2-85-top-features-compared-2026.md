# Jabra Evolve2 85: Top Features Compared (2026)

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/jabra-evolve2-85-top-features-compared-2026/)

You're on a call in a shared office or a coffee shop. And the person on the other end keeps asking you to repeat yourself because of the background noise. That's the exact problem the Jabra Evolve2 85 is built to solve. It's a professional-grade wireless headset with a charging stand, and the specs tell a fairly specific story about who it's meant for. Three things stand out: the noise cancellation and microphone array, the battery life paired with the included charging stand, and the physical design - over-ear, foldable, with a hidden boom arm. Each comes with a trade-off worth understanding before you commit. Noise cancellation and the 10-microphone array The Evolve2 85 uses [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)we found that Jabra claims cancels 54% more ambient noise than the previous Evolve generation. That figure comes from Jabra's own comparison. So treat it as a relative improvement within the product line rather than an absolute measurement against every competitor. What matters practically is that the [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)what caught our attention from what we can tell at this level is designed for open-plan offices, airport lounges, and commutes - environments with steady, low-frequency drone like HVAC hum, engine noise, or distant chatter. It won't eliminate a colleague talking directly next to you, and no [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)we found we like that headset reliably does. The more interesting number is the microphone array: 10 built-in microphones. Jabra claims this delivers 97% less background conversation noise on calls. That's a lot of mics for a headset, and the practical effect is beamforming - the headset focuses on your voice and rejects sound coming from other directions. If you take calls from noisy spaces. This is the most valuable feature here. Call quality is what separates a business headset from a pair of consumer headphones, and 10 mics is a serious investment in that direction. The trade-off: [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) and aggressive microphone processing can make your own voice sound slightly processed to you through sidetone, and heavy noise rejection sometimes clips the natural rhythm of speech. Some people notice this; others don't. There's also no way to know from the spec sheet how the [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) performs against specific competitors - Jabra only gives you the relative 54% figure. If you've used [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headphones before and found them underwhelming in very loud environments, this won't be a magic fix, but it should be a meaningful step up from a non-[ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headset. Battery life and the charging stand Jabra rates the Evolve2 85 at up to 37 hours on a single charge. That's a full workweek of eight-hour days with hours to spare, or roughly four to five days of typical office use if you're not on calls constantly. For a wireless headset, this is genuinely long - most consumer [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headphones land in the 20-30 hour range, and many business headsets fall short of that. The charging stand changes how you use it. Instead of plugging in a cable, you drop the headset on the stand at your desk, and it charges between calls. Combined with 37 hours of runtime. This means you can largely stop thinking about battery. A full charge takes 2.5 hours, which is on the slower side - but with a stand, you're topping up in short bursts rather than waiting for a full cycle, so the charge time matters less than it would for a headset you plug in once a day. The trade-off is portability logistics. The stand lives on a desk, so if you're moving between home and office, you're either carrying the stand or relying on the [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) cable. There's a 3.5mm jack cable and a carry case included, so wired use is possible when the battery dies, but the charging stand is a desk accessory, not a travel one. If your work is fully mobile - different locations every day - the stand's value drops substantially. Design. Boom arm - and fit This is an over-ear headset with memory foam padding and a foldable design. Over-ear means the cups surround your ears rather than pressing on them, which generally distributes pressure better over long sessions than on-ear designs. The memory foam is listed as the enclosure material, which is a slightly odd way to phrase it - what it really tells you is that the ear cushions use memory foam, which conforms to your head shape rather than staying rigid. The hidden boom arm is the design centerpiece. When you're not on a call, the boom arm folds away into the headset body, so it looks like a normal pair of over-ear headphones. When you need to talk, you pull it out. This is a deliberate choice for people who don't want a microphone dangling in front of their face during focus time. For everyday listening. The trade-off is mechanical: a foldable boom arm is one more moving part that can wear over time, and some users find boom arms less natural than a minimal beamforming mic built into the earcup. Jabra also notes an improved busylight that's more visible than before - a red light that signals to people around you that you're on a call. In an office, that's a small but genuinely useful attribute. The foldable design and included carry case suggest Jabra expects some travel use, even if the charging stand is desk-bound. The headset weighs 2 pounds according to the listing, which is heavy for headphones - typical over-ear [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headphones weigh under a pound. That figure may feature packaging or be inaccurate, but if the headset itself is anywhere near that weight, it will feel substantial on your head over long sessions. In real-world use. This is worth flagging because the spec sheet doesn't give a clear standalone headset weight. Connectivity and compatibility [Bluetooth 5](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) with a rated range of 98 feet is solid. [Bluetooth 5](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) improves connection stability and power efficiency over older versions, and 98 feet means you can walk to a printer or kitchen without dropping the call. Real-world range will be less through walls. But for open office or home use, it's more than enough. The bigger connectivity story is the included Jabra Link 380c [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) Bluetooth adapter. This is what makes the headset UC-certified - it works with Microsoft Teams, Cisco, Mitel, Avaya - and other unified communications platforms. For most people, the adapter plugs into your computer and gives you a dedicated wireless link, which typically delivers better call quality and lower latency than connecting directly via your computer's Bluetooth. If you use a softphone or a UC platform for work, this adapter is the difference between a headset that works and one that works well. The listing says "All Platforms" for the style, and the headset is described as UC-certified with a Microsoft Teams-certified variant available - note that this specific listing may or may not be the Teams variant, so check if that matters to you. Wired options are covered: a 1.2m [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) to USB-A cable for charging and a 1.2m 3.5mm jack audio cable for wired listening. The 3.5mm cable means you can use the headset with devices that don't support Bluetooth, including in-flight entertainment systems and older audio gear. Water resistance is listed, though no IP rating is given, so treat it as splash-resistant rather than something you'd wear in heavy rain. Sound and controls The 40mm dynamic drivers cover a frequency range of 20 Hz to 20 kHz, which is the standard audible range for human hearing. Nothing here suggests audiophile tuning - this is a business headset first - and the "leak-tolerate" speaker design is about keeping sound in and noise out rather than delivering a wide soundstage. For calls. Podcasts, and background music during work, it's appropriate. For key music listening. It's not the priority. Impedance is 32 ohms, which is low and means the headset is uncomplicated to drive from a phone or laptop without a separate amplifier - relevant only if you're using the wired connection. Controls are touch-based media controls. Touch controls on headphones are polarizing: they keep the design clean but can be finicky, especially with gloves or cold fingers, and accidental taps happen. There's no physical button mentioned for volume or track control. So if you prefer tactile buttons, this is a compromise. What the data doesn't tell you A few things are missing or unclear. The headset weight is listed at 2 pounds, which is either inaccurate or means the product is unusually heavy - a standalone headset weight isn't given. The [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) performance is only described relative to Jabra's previous generation, not against competitors. There's no IP rating despite water resistance being listed. On the practical side. The specific UC certification for this exact listing isn't confirmed beyond "All Platforms" and the mention of a Teams variant elsewhere. And the customer rating sits at 4.1 out of 5 from 114 reviews, which is solid but not exceptional - enough to suggest most buyers are satisfied, not enough to call it universally loved. Summary: The Jabra Evolve2 85 is a professional wireless headset built around call clarity, long battery life, and a discreet design that hides the microphone when you're not using it. Its strengths are the 10-microphone array, 37-hour battery with a desk charging stand, and UC certification with the included [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) adapter - all of which matter if you spend your day on calls in shared or noisy spaces. The trade-offs are the desk-bound charging stand, the touch controls, the unclear weight, and [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) performance that's only described relative to Jabra's own previous model. If you need a headset for music-first listening or fully mobile use without a desk, this isn't the obvious pick. Who this is for This headset suits people who take a lot of calls from a fixed desk - remote workers. Open-office employees, sales and support staff, and anyone whose day runs through Microsoft Teams, Cisco, or another UC platform. The combination of the Link 380c adapter. 10 microphones, and the charging stand is designed for that workflow. It also fits people who want one headset for both calls and music, and who value not having a boom mic in front of their face during focus time. The 37-hour battery and 98-foot Bluetooth range make it practical for all-day wear without constant charging. Who should look elsewhere If you work from cafés, trains, or multiple locations daily, the charging stand becomes dead weight and you'd be better served by a headset designed around portability. If music quality is your priority alongside calls, the 40mm drivers and business-first tuning suggest you'd get more from a consumer [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headphone. If you dislike touch controls or need physical buttons for volume and track skipping, look for a model with tactile controls. And if the 2-pound weight figure is accurate for the headset itself. Anyone sensitive to headset pressure over long sessions should verify the actual weight before buying. Finally.
