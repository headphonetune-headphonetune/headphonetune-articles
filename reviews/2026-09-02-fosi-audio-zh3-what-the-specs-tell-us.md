# Fosi Audio ZH3: What the Specs Tell Us

what caught our attention the Fosi Audio ZH3 is a desktop component, not a portable device, so forget commuting or working out. The realistic scenario is sitting at a desk, at a computer, with a pair of wired headphones and a stack of gear. The ZH3 is trying to be the single box that replaces two or three others - and the specs suggest it does that with some genuine ambition. The Core Chipset and What It Means The heart of the ZH3 is the AKM4493SEQ DAC chip paired with the XMOS XU316 processor. The AKM chip is a well-regarded. Modern converter that handles [high-resolution audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/) formats without breaking a sweat. The XMOS chip is the workhorse for USB input, handling the data stream. For everyday listening, the real-world takeaway is that this isn't a budget, off-the-shelf DAC implementation. The components are chosen for performance. And they aren't the cheapest options available. This matters because the DAC is the part of the chain that turns digital data into an analog signal, and a better chip here means less distortion and a cleaner signal path, which you hear as detail and separation in the music. The listed specs of PCM 768kHz/32-bit and DSD512 support are the maximums. Not the daily operating point. Most streaming services top out at 24-bit/192kHz* (based on available data), and CDs are 16-bit/44.1kHz* (based on available data). What this high ceiling means is that the ZH3 will not be a bottleneck for years. In real-world use, it isn't that you will notice a difference between 192kHz* (based on available data) and 768kHz files-you almost certainly won't-but it's that the chipset is running well below its limits during normal use, which generally translates to lower noise and better linearity in the audible range. It is a "future-proofing" spec that also indicates the internal design is not being stressed by everyday content. Output Power and Headphone Compatibility This is where the ZH3 separates itself from smaller, portable DAC dongles. The single-ended 6.35mm output delivers 640mW into 32 ohms. And the balanced 4.4mm output delivers a hefty 2570mW into 32 ohms. To put that in perspective, 640mW is enough to drive the vast majority of full-size over-ear headphones to loud, healthy levels. The 2570mW figure is for demanding. High-end planars or inefficient dynamic drivers that need serious current to sound their best. For most people, the 3-level gain switch isn't just a marketing bullet point. it's a practical necessity. A high-output amp with a sensitive in-ear monitor (IEM) will produce a constant hiss and make volume adjustments impossible because even the lowest setting is too loud. The low gain setting tames the output for IEMs. The medium setting works for most dynamic headphones, and the high setting is for the power-hungry planars. The spec sheet claims support for 16Ω to 300Ω headphones, which covers basically every headphone on the market, but the gain switch is what makes that range usable without background noise or a jumpy volume knob. If you own both sensitive IEMs and inefficient over-ears, this flexibility is the difference between a useful device and a frustrating one. On the practical side. The Balanced Output and the 4.4mm Connection The 4.4mm balanced output is not just about more power. Balanced connections double the amplifier circuitry per channel, which can lower crosstalk and improve channel separation. The spec lists 117dB crosstalk, which is excellent. Practically, you get a wider, more precise stereo image if your headphones have a balanced cable. Many high-end headphones come with a balanced cable in the box or offer it as an aftermarket option. The ZH3 gives you the choice. If you never use a balanced cable, you're leaving some headroom on the table, but the single-ended output is still strong enough for most users. The key point is that the option is there. And the implementation is serious, not a token addition. Noise Floor and Distortion The noise floor of ≤1.9μV and THD+N of ≤0.00018% are the quietest and cleanest numbers you will see outside of esoteric, high-end gear. What do these numbers mean? The noise floor is the background hiss you hear when no music is playing. At 1.9μV, that hiss is effectively inaudible with any headphone, including ultra-sensitive IEMs. For comparison, this is the spec that makes the low gain setting for IEMs actually work. The distortion figure means the amp is adding nearly nothing to the signal. it's a measure of accuracy. you're hearing the recording and the headphones, not the amplifier's coloration. For a listener who wants a neutral. Transparent chain, these numbers are reassuring. For someone who wants a "warm" or "tube-like" sound, this amp isn't that. In daily use. It's clean, precise, and uncolored. The Preamp Function and Outputs The ZH3 isn't just a headphone amp. It has RCA and XLR outputs that can feed powered speakers or a separate power amplifier. The XLR outputs are balanced, which is a feature usually found on far more expensive units. This makes the ZH3 a true preamp, allowing you to switch between headphones and speakers without unplugging cables. There is a important note in the specs: "RCA and XLR output levels differ considerably; please select according to your backend equipment." This is a warning that the XLR outputs are at a higher voltage (likely +4dBu professional level) than the RCA outputs (likely -10dBV consumer level). If you connect the XLR outputs to a consumer amp that expects a lower input level. You will get a very loud, potentially distorted signal. You need to know what your powered speakers or amp expect. If you have pro-audio monitors with XLR inputs. The balanced output is ideal. If you have consumer gear, stick with the RCA outs. This is not a flaw. But it is a unboxing consideration that requires a bit of knowledge about your other equipment. EQ and Filters: The Customization Options The ZH3 includes bass and treble EQ adjustments. Six digital filter types, and two swappable op-amps. For typical buyers, the EQ is a simple shelf adjustment, not a multi-band parametric equalizer. it's for fine-tuning a headphone's tonality. Not for correcting deep frequency response issues. The six filters are digital reconstruction filters that subtly alter the sound's transient response and roll-off. Some listeners can hear differences in the "sharp" vs. "gradual" filter settings, but for many, the effect is minimal. The swappable op-amps are a more involved modification. You can pull out the stock OPA1612 chips and install different models to change the sound signature. This is a niche hobbyist capability. It allows for tinkering, but it also voids the warranty and requires a fundamental understanding of electronics. The crucial note here is that "EQ only works with headphone output." The EQ does not affect the RCA or XLR preamp outputs. This makes sense-you want to adjust the sound for your headphones, but you do not want to color the signal going to your speakers or main amp. It is a thoughtful design choice that prevents accidental tone-shaping of your main system. For everyday listening. The screen-based control system means you navigate menus to change these settings, which is fine for occasional adjustments but not something you will do frequently. Inputs and Connectivity The ZH3 offers USB, optical, coaxial, and RCA inputs. The USB input is the primary connection for a computer, and the XMOS XU316 chip handles it with low latency and high stability. The optical and coaxial inputs allow connection to a TV, CD player, or game console. The RCA input is an analog passthrough, which means you can connect a turntable with a built-in phono preamp or another analog source and route it through the ZH3's volume control and headphone amp. The UAC mode switching (USB Audio Class 1.0 and 2.0) is a practical feature for gaming consoles. Many consoles only support UAC 1.0, which is limited to 24-bit/96kHz* (based on available data). Switching to UAC 1.0 mode ensures the ZH3 is recognized by the console and passes audio without issues. This is a specific but useful attribute for gamers who want to use a high-quality DAC with a PlayStation or Switch. It isn't a selling point for everyone, but for those with a console on the desk, it's a reason to choose this over a DAC that lacks the switch. Physical Design and Build The item weight is 2.9 pounds. This is a substantial, solid piece of desktop gear. In real-world use. It will not slide around easily, and the metal chassis suggests durability. The screen is integrated into the front panel for settings and volume display. The "Z-Series design language" suggests a cohesive aesthetic, but without seeing it in person, the visual appeal is subjective. What matters is that the chassis is heavy enough to stay put when you plug or unplug cables. And the connectors are likely to be mounted securely to the case, reducing stress on the internal circuit board. The Missing Information The spec sheet doesn't provide dimensions. The warranty is listed as 24 months, which is a solid, above-average commitment from the manufacturer. It covers the product for two years, which is a solid sign for build confidence. The The ZH3 is not a budget device. it's positioned as a mid-range all-in-one fix. The question is whether the convenience of having a DAC, headphone amp, and preamp in one box justifies the cost compared to buying separate units. The advantage of the all-in-one is fewer cables. Less desk clutter, and a single volume control for both headphones and speakers. For most people, the disadvantage is that the if one component fails, you lose everything, and you can't upgrade one part without replacing the whole unit. The ZH3 mitigates this by offering a solid implementation of each function, not a compromised one. The balanced XLR preamp outputs and the high-power balanced headphone output are features you would typically need to buy separate units to get. For someone with a computer, a pair of capable headphones, and powered speakers, the ZH3 replaces a separate DAC and headphone amp and supplies a volume-controlled preamp for the speakers. it's a clean. Single-box solution that doesn't sacrifice performance for convenience. The swappable op-amps and extensive filter options are extras that some will never touch, but they don't complicate the basic operation. On the practical side, the unit works well out of the box with default settings. Summary: The Fosi Audio ZH3 is a serious all-in-one desktop audio hub that combines a high-resolution DAC. A capable headphone amplifier, and a balanced preamp into a single 2.9-pound chassis. it's built around quality components-AKM4493SEQ. XMOS XU316, and four OPA1612 op-amps-and delivers clean, transparent sound with a low noise floor suitable for sensitive IEMs and enough power for demanding over-ear headphones. The XLR preamp outputs and UAC switching for consoles make it versatile beyond a uncomplicated headphone amp. The main trade-off is that you're buying an integrated unit. So future upgrades mean replacing the whole device, and the EQ only works on the headphone output, not the preamp outs. For a desk unboxing with a computer, headphones, and active speakers, this is a well-considered solution. Who this is for This is for the desktop audio enthusiast who wants a single, high-quality box to drive both headphones and powered speakers without a multi-feature chain of separate components. It suits someone who owns a mix of sensitive in-ear monitors and power-hungry planar headphones. Because the three-level gain switch handles both extremes cleanly. If you value a clean, transparent, uncolored sound and want the flexibility to adjust gain and EQ via a screen rather than physical switches, this is a strong candidate.
