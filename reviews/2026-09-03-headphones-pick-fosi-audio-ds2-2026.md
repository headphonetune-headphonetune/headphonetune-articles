# Headphones Pick: Fosi Audio DS2 (2026)

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/headphones-pick-fosi-audio-ds2-2026/)

The Fosi Audio DS2 is a petite [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) dongle that sits between your phone or computer and your wired headphones. It's a DAC (digital-to-analog converter) and headphone amplifier in one. And its job is to take the digital audio signal from your device and turn it into a clean analog signal your headphones can use. Most phones and laptops already do this internally. But the quality of that built-in circuitry is often mediocre. The DS2 is trying to be a better version of that. The first thing that stands out is the dual output. This thing has both a standard 3.5mm single-ended jack and a 4.4mm balanced jack. That is not typical for a dongle at this level. Most portable DACs in this size range offer only the 3.5mm output. And if you want balanced, you usually have to step up to a larger, more expensive desktop unit or a bulkier portable amp. Having both on a device this petite is genuinely useful if you own headphones with a 4.4mm balanced cable. Because balanced outputs typically deliver more power and better channel separation. The spec sheet claims 170mW@32Ω per channel. Which is above average for a USB dongle. For context, many comparable dongles in this class put out somewhere around 70-100mW into 32 ohms. That extra headroom means you can drive harder-to-power over-ear headphones, not just sensitive in-ear monitors. If you have a pair of planar magnetics or high-impedance dynamic headphones, this will likely get them to a listenable volume without clipping, though it won't replace a proper desktop amp for the most demanding cans. The balanced output is the real differentiator here. Compared to similar products in the same price range, most of which are single-ended only, the DS2 offers a meaningful upgrade path. If you already have a balanced cable for your headphones, you can use this dongle to get more clean power out of a phone or laptop. If you don't have balanced cables. The 3.5mm output is still solid, but you're leaving some of the device's potential on the table. Under the hood. There are two Cirrus Logic CS43131 DAC chips. One for each channel. For everyday listening, this is a common architecture in higher-end portable DACs, and it's a good sign. Dual DACs allow for true differential output. Which theoretically reduces crosstalk and improves dynamic range. The measured specs back this up: a noise floor of 1μV. Total harmonic distortion at 0.0001%. A signal-to-noise ratio of 130dB, and SINAD (signal-to-noise and distortion ratio) of 109dB. These numbers are outstanding. For comparison, many phones with a headphone jack measure SINAD in the 80-90dB* (based on available data) range, and even some dedicated DACs struggle to hit 100dB. The DS2 is objectively cleaner than what most people are used to. In practical terms. This means you won't hear background hiss, and quiet passages in music won't be smeared by electronic noise. If you're using sensitive in-ear monitors, the ultra-low noise floor is especially noticeable; you'll hear silence when there's silence, not a faint static buzz. The DS2 handles DSD256 and PCM up to 32bit/384kHz. that's [high-resolution audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/) territory. Whether you actually need that's a separate question. Most streaming services like Spotify and Apple Music top out at CD quality (16bit/44.1kHz* (based on available data)) or slightly higher. Tidal and Qobuz offer hi-res files, but the difference between 24bit/96kHz* (based on available data) and 32bit/384kHz is largely inaudible to human ears. Still. Having the decoding capability means the DAC will handle any file you throw at it without downsampling or errors. It's a "future-proofing" characteristic more than a daily necessity, but it doesn't hurt. Battery life is a non-issue here because the DS2 draws power from the source device. It doesn't have an internal battery. That's typical for this category. But worth noting because some competing dongles do offer a battery to isolate power from the phone. In real-world use, the DS2 is powered over [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c). Which means it will drain your phone's battery slightly faster than just plugging headphones directly in. The spec sheet claims it does this efficiently. And the dual CS43131 chips are known for relatively low power draw. But you should expect maybe 5-10% additional battery drain per hour of listening on a phone. On a laptop, this is irrelevant. The physical design is CNC-machined aluminum. Which gives it a solid, premium feel compared to the plastic dongles that ship with many phones. It weighs 1.92 ounces. Which is light enough to be portable but heavier than a bare cable. If you're clipping this to a keychain or letting it dangle from your phone, the metal body adds a bit of heft. For most people. It's not a issue, but it's not weightless either. The build quality appears to be above average for the category, and the metal casing likely helps with heat dissipation. DACs can get warm during extended use. And aluminum pulls heat away better than plastic. One notable attribute is the independent 60-step volume control with physical buttons. This is more useful than it sounds. Most dongles rely on the source device's volume control. Which often lacks fine granularity. With the DS2, you get precise control over output level, and it has volume memory, meaning it remembers where you left off when you unplug and reconnect. This is a practical capability for anyone who switches between sensitive IEMs and power-hungry over-ears, because you can set a well-fitting level for each and not have to readjust from scratch every time. The buttons are on the device itself. Which is fine when you're sitting at a desk, but slightly awkward if the dongle is in your pocket. You'll need to fish it out to change volume. Though you can also use the source device's volume as a master control if you prefer. Compatibility is broad. The DS2 works with iPhone 15 series (including Pro and Pro Max), iPad Pro, Android phones with [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)we could see. And computers running Windows or macOS. On the practical side. This isn't a niche product. It also functions as an external sound card for laptops, which is a fine bonus if your laptop's built-in audio jack is noisy or lacks power. The listing mentions support for iPhone 15 specifically. Which makes sense because Apple's newer phones have [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) but no headphone jack. If you're an iPhone 15 user with a collection of wired headphones. This is one of the more straightforward ways to get high-quality audio out of your phone. For Android users, the DS2 should work plug-and-play, but note that some Android phones have quirks with USB audio output, and you may need to enable USB audio routing in developer settings or use a USB audio player pro app to bypass the system's resampling. In everyday use, that's not a flaw of the DS2; it's an Android ecosystem issue. For comparison, the warranty is 24 months from the manufacturer, which is above the typical one-year warranty you see on many electronics. That suggests the company stands behind the product. The customer reviews show 4.4 out of 5 stars from 455 ratings. Which is decent but not stellar. Some users likely had compatibility issues or expected a different sound signature. The DS2 is designed to be neutral and transparent, so if you're used to a warm, colored sound from your current unboxing, this might sound clinical to you initially. That's not a defect; it's a design choice. In daily use, the DS2 is meant to reproduce the recording as accurately as possible, not to flatter it. Compared to similar products in its class, the DS2 sits above the typical inexpensive dongle in both build quality and measured performance. It competes with devices like the Apple [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) dongle (which is decent but limited in power) and other budget DACs from brands like Moondrop or Fiio. And even then, the difference would be inaudible in most real-world listening situations. What's missing from the data? For typical buyers. That's a common limitation in this category, but worth considering if you're rough on your gear. For real-world use. Think about your listening habits. If you're commuting with IEMs and a phone. The DS2 adds a petite metal brick to your cable chain. It's not as time-saving as a pair of wireless earbuds, but you're buying this because you care about sound quality over convenience. If you're at a desk with a laptop. The DS2 is a compact way to upgrade your computer's audio without occupying desk space. The dual outputs mean you can leave a 3.5mm headphone plugged in for daily use and switch to a balanced cable when you want to listen critically. The volume buttons give you tactile control without having to look at a screen. For everyday listening, the power output of 170mW into 32 ohms is sufficient for most dynamic headphones. It will drive something like a Sennheiser HD600 (300 ohms) to reasonable volumes. Though not with the same authority as a desktop amp. It will easily drive low-impedance planar headphones like the Audeze LCD-1 or the Hifiman Sundara. Which typically need more current. If you have very inefficient headphones, like some of the harder-to-drive planars from Hifiman or Audeze, this dongle will get them loud but might not fully control the bass response. That's the trade-off of a portable device; you can't fit a desktop-sized amplifier into a pocket-sized enclosure and expect identical performance. One thing to consider is that the DS2 does not have a built-in battery. In real-world use. This means it relies entirely on the source device for power. On a phone. This is fine for short listening sessions, but if you're planning a long flight or a full day of use, your phone battery will take a hit. On a laptop, this is a non-issue. Some competing products. Like the AudioQuest DragonFly series, also draw power from the source, so this isn't unusual, but it's a limitation to be aware of if you're a heavy mobile user. The 4.4mm balanced output is the attribute that makes this dongle stand out. If you don't have any balanced cables. You can still use the 3.5mm output, and it will sound solid, but you're basically paying for a characteristic you're not using. If you do have balanced headphones, this is one of the few space-saving, reasonably priced options that lets you take advantage of them on the go. That alone justifies the purchase for some people. Looking at value. The DS2 offers a lot of measured performance for its price point. The build quality is solid. The specs are top-notch, and the dual outputs provide flexibility. The main competitors in this space are dongles that cost less but lack the balanced output and the dual DAC architecture. Or dongles that cost more but offer marginal improvements that most people can't hear. The DS2 sits in a sweet spot for anyone who wants a genuine upgrade over their phone's built-in audio without jumping into the world of separate portable DAPs or desktop stacks. If you notice hiss in quiet passages, lack of bass control, or just feel like your headphones aren't being driven to their full potential, the DS2 will give you a clear, measurable improvement. It's also for the headphone enthusiast who owns a 4.4mm balanced cable and wants to use it on the go without carrying a bulky amplifier.
