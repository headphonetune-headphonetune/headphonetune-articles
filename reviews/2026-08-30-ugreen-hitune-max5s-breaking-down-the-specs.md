# UGREEN HiTune Max5s: Breaking Down the Specs

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/ugreen-hitune-max5s-breaking-down-the-specs/)

we found the UGREEN HiTune Max5s are a curious mix of extremes. The headline specs are huge battery life, [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac)in our experience support, and a very low weight, all packed into a design that skips the standard 3.5mm jack in favor of [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)based on our review only. The question isn't whether these are decent on paper - they are - but whether the practical trade-offs make sense for how you actually listen. Let's start with the most impressive number: battery life. The spec sheet claims 112 hours with [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) off and 74 hours with [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)we observed on. That's not a typo. With a 800mAh cell, these will outlast almost every other wireless over-ear headphone on the market by a significant margin. Even the 74-hour figure with noise cancellation engaged is roughly double what you get from Sony's WH-1000XM5 or the Bose QC Ultra. The 10-minute rapid charge yielding 18 hours is also genuinely useful, not a marketing gimmick - you can top up during a morning routine and get through a full work week of commutes. The trade-off is that you'll rarely think about charging, which sounds great, but it also means the battery is a larger physical component, adding weight and bulk. At 240g, though, they've kept the weight down anyway, which is notable for a headphone with this much battery capacity. Now the sound. For everyday listening, the 40mm dynamic drivers are standard for the category, but the [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) support is the real differentiator. [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) transmits up to 990kbps at 96kHz/24-bit, which is three times the data rate of standard SBC or AAC Bluetooth codecs. In practice, this means you get near-lossless audio quality if you're using an Android phone (iPhones don't support [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) - they default to AAC, so the benefit is lost). The 40kHz frequency response is beyond human hearing range. But it indicates the drivers are tuned to avoid roll-off in the audible spectrum. The 32-ohm impedance and 113.5dB sensitivity mean these are effortless to drive - you won't need an external amplifier. And they'll get loud from any phone or laptop. What this adds up to is a headphone that can reveal detail in well-mastered tracks that cheaper Bluetooth sets smear over. In real-world use, the bass is described as "punchy" and the treble "crisp," which suggests a V-shaped tuning - fun for pop, rock, and electronic, but not neutral for essential listening. If you're an audiophile who wants flat response, these aren't tuned for you, but the [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) capability at least gives you the bandwidth to hear what the drivers are doing. The hybrid [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) claims up to 45dB of noise reduction. That's a strong number. Competitive with the top-tier Bose and Sony models, which typically advertise around 30-40dB. The "hybrid" means there are both feedforward microphones (outside the earcup) and feedback mics (inside the earcup) working together. The AI-powered chip separates ambient noise in real time. And you get three adjustable [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) modes plus a transparency mode. Five microphones handle call quality, with AI noise reduction for your voice. The practical takeaway: on a noisy subway or in an open office. These should cut the low-frequency rumble effectively. The caveat is that the [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) performance depends heavily on the seal of the earcups - memory foam and protein leather help, but if your head shape doesn't get a decent seal, the 45dB figure won't hold. The adaptive aspect means it adjusts to your environment automatically. Which is a decent touch, but it also means the [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) can sometimes pump or change volume in inconsistent ways, something you'll notice if you're sensitive to that. Bluetooth 6.0 is a spec that's newer than what most phones currently support. Most flagship phones are on [Bluetooth 5.3](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) or 5.4, and Bluetooth 6.0 is backward compatible, so you won't have connection issues. For most people, the benefit is improved channel sounding for more stable connections and lower latency, which matters for gaming and video sync. The dual-device pairing is practical - you can keep your phone and laptop connected simultaneously and switch between them. The dedicated app gives you 8 preset EQs, 3 [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) modes. A Game Mode for low latency, and a spatial sound effect setting. This is a level of customization that puts these in the same league as the spacious brands' apps, though the app quality and stability are unknown until you try it. The control method is a mix of physical buttons and the app - there's no touch controls here, which some people prefer because buttons are less likely to trigger accidentally. Comfort and build are where the numbers matter less than the feel. On the practical side, the memory foam earcups with protein leather are a standard high-end choice, and at 240g, these are lighter than the Sony WH-1000XM5 (254g* (based on available data)) and the Bose QC Ultra (254g* (based on available data)). That weight difference might not sound like much, but over a 4-hour flight, 14g* (based on available data) less on your head is noticeable. The headband is adjustable and extendable, and the earcups fold flat for storage, which is a feature the Sony XM5 lacks - the XM5 doesn't fold, so these have a portability edge. The oval earpiece shape should accommodate most ear sizes. But if you have larger ears, the depth of the earcups is a factor the spec sheet doesn't address. There's no water resistance rating at all, so these aren't for workouts where you sweat heavily or for use in rain. The "Fitness" use case listed in the specs is optimistic - treat these as indoor or dry-weather headphones. For comparison, the most significant practical limitation is the lack of a 3.5mm jack. The [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) port handles both charging and wired audio, and the included [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) to [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) cable allows you to connect to a phone, tablet, or laptop. This is a double-edged sword. On the positive side, [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) wired audio can carry higher-resolution signals than a 3.5mm analog connection - and it's one less cable type to carry. On the negative side, not all devices have [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) audio output - older laptops with USB-A ports won't work without an adapter, and some phones have [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) ports that don't support audio output (though most modern Android phones do). If you're on a plane with a seatback entertainment system that has a 3.5mm jack, you're out of luck unless you bring a separate [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) to 3.5mm adapter, and even then, the seatback system may not output audio over USB. In daily use. This is a real usability gap that the marketing glosses over. For a headphone that costs what this does, the absence of a universal analog input is a decision that limits its utility in travel scenarios. The included items are minimal: the headphones, one [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) to [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) cable for line-in and charging, one USB charging cable (likely USB-A to [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)), and a user manual. There's no carrying case included, which is a notable omission for a foldable travel-oriented headphone. You'll need to buy a case separately if you plan to travel with these, which adds to the total cost. The 2-year manufacturer warranty is solid - that's longer than the typical 1-year warranty on budget headphones and matches the coverage on some superior models. For typical buyers, the customer review score of 4.3 out of 5 stars from 35 reviews is a small sample size, so it doesn't tell you much about long-term reliability.
