# POWMEE M2 Kids: Specs &amp; Performance Review

I noticed that based on our review a kid on a cross-country flight, a parent who has already lost two pairs of earbuds to the seat-back pocket, and a tablet loaded with downloaded movies. That's the scenario the POWMEE M2 was built for, and the specs back it up: a 3.5mm wired connection, a foldable on-ear frame, a nylon-braided cord, and a 40mm dynamic driver. It's a kids' headphone, and it's priced like one. The question is whether the hardware justifies the buy, and the data gives a fairly clear answer. Key spec highlights: 40mm dynamic drivers (large for this category), 20Hz-20kHz frequency range, 3.5mm wired jack only, 4.2-foot nylon-braided cable, foldable on-ear design, adjustable headband with protein cushion earcups, 24-month warranty, and a 0.21kg weight. The review record is strong - 4.7 stars across 10,819 ratings. For everyday listening, the spec sheet is inconsistent in a few places (more on that below), but the core aspect set is coherent for the intended use. Start with the driver size, because it's the most meaningful audio spec here. 40mm dynamic drivers are unusually large for on-ear headphones at any price, let alone a kids' pair. Most budget on-ears sit in the 30-36mm* (based on available data) range. A bigger driver moves more air, which generally translates to stronger bass response and less distortion at higher volumes. For a child listening to music, watching movies, or playing games on a tablet, that extra headroom matters - dialogue stays clear, soundtracks don't compress into mush, and the headphones don't need to be cranked as high to be audible. In real-world use, the 20Hz-20kHz frequency range is the standard human hearing range and is effectively a box-tick spec; every headphone claims it. The driver size is the number that actually tells you something. That said, wired on-ear headphones at this price won't deliver audiophile sound. The 40mm driver helps, but the enclosure is nylon, the earcups are described as "protein" (a synthetic leather alternative), and there's no mention of impedance or sensitivity - two specs that would tell you how effortless the headphones are to drive and how loud they'll get from a phone or tablet. Their absence isn't a red flag at this price point, but it does mean you're relying on the driver size and the review record rather than a complete electrical picture. The wired-only design is the single biggest trade-off, and it's worth being blunt about it. There's no Bluetooth, no battery, no charging. The headphones work when plugged in and don't work when not. For a child, that's a feature: no pairing headaches, no dead battery mid-flight, no lost charging cable. Plug it into a tablet, a Kindle, a laptop, a car DVD player, or an in-flight entertainment system and it just works. The 4.2-foot nylon-braided cord is long enough to reach from a seat-back screen to a kid's head without dangling into the aisle, and nylon braiding is meaningfully more long-lasting than the rubber-coated cords that tend to split at the strain relief after a few months of tugging. Tangle-free is a marketing phrase, but braided nylon genuinely tangles less than smooth rubber. For most people, the flip side: the spec sheet lists "wireless technology: Bluetooth" in the technical specifications. That appears to be a data-entry error - nothing in the features, description, or box contents mentions Bluetooth, and a wired 3.5mm headphone with a 4.2-foot cord doesn't have a wireless mode. Buyers should treat this as a wired-only product. The same caution applies to "control type: Volume Control" and "control method: Touch." There's no inline remote described anywhere in the characteristic list, and touch controls on a kids' headphone would be unusual. The "controller type: Integrated Controls" line is equally vague. If inline volume or track control matters to you, don't assume it's there - the data doesn't confirm it. Compatibility is where the 3.5mm jack becomes both an advantage and a limitation. Any device with a standard 3.5mm headphone jack works: most Android phones, Chromebooks, iPads and iPhones up to the 6S era, iPods, laptops, tablets, desktop computers, car DVD players, and seat-back entertainment systems on most airlines. For iPhone 7 and later, and for 2020 iPad Pro 11 and 12.9 models, you'll need a Lightning-to-3.5mm adapter, which is not included. That's a real consideration if your household is all-Apple and current-generation. A Lightning adapter is a small extra buy, but it's one more thing to lose, and it's worth knowing before you buy rather than after. The foldable design and 0.21kg weight are the two specs that matter most for portability, and they work together. Foldable on-ear headphones collapse into a roughly palm-sized package that slides into a backpack pocket or a suitcase corner. At 0.21kg (about 7.4 ounces), these are light enough that a child won't complain about neck strain or ear fatigue during a long trip. That said, the adjustable headband extends to fit different head sizes, which matters because kids grow - a pair that fits a 5-year-old snugly may need to stretch for an 11-year-previous, and the adjustment range handles that. The "age 3 and up" claim in the aspect list is worth taking with a grain of salt: on-ear headphones on a 3-year-established are a choking hazard waiting to happen if the earcups detach, and the 4.2-foot cord is a strangulation risk for very miniature children. Realistically, this is a better fit for ages 5-12. Noise isolation is listed as a capability, and the soft cushion earcups do give you passive isolation - they physically block some ambient sound. But "noise control: Sound Isolation" isn't [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). There's no battery, no [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) circuitry, and no mention of [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)in my opinion anywhere in the specs. On a plane, these will take the edge off engine drone but won't silence it. For a child, that's safer - they can still hear a parent or a flight attendant - but if you're expecting the quiet of a pair of [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)I noticed that in our experience headphones, you'll be disappointed. Water resistance is explicitly "Not Water Resistant." That's a meaningful limitation for kids. Sweat, spilled juice, a rain-soaked walk to school, or a dropped water bottle in a backpack will all be problems. On the practical side, these aren't gym headphones and not outdoor-all-weather headphones. Keep them dry. The 24-month warranty is a genuine bright spot. Two years of coverage on a kids' product is above average, and the feature text notes that customer service is "very straightforward to reach." For a product that will be folded, unfolded, tugged, dropped, and shoved into bags by a child, a two-year warranty is worth more than it would be on an adult's desk headphones. If the cord frays or a hinge gives out at month 18, you have recourse. The review record - 4.7 stars from 10,819 ratings - is the strongest single data point in the entire spec sheet. That's a large sample size and a high average, and it suggests the durability claims hold up in practice more often than not. A product with 10,000+ reviews at 4.7 stars has survived the scrutiny of a lot of parents and kids. It doesn't guarantee your pair will be optimal, but it's a better predictor than any single spec. Where the data is thin: there's no impedance or sensitivity rating, so it's hard to say how loud these get from a low-output device like a Kindle or an older phone. There's no mention of a microphone, so don't count on these for calls or online classes that need two-way audio. The "theme: Video Game" tag and "specific uses: Travel, Music, Gaming, Education" are category tags rather than meaningful specs, but they do confirm the intended use cases. For comparison, the "Retractable" aspect tag is confusing - there's nothing in the description about a retractable cord, and the 4.2-foot braided cord is described as fixed. Treat that as another data error. The "in-ear" style label is also wrong. These are on-ear headphones with rounded earcup tips, not in-ear buds.
