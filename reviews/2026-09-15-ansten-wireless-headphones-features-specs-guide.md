Here's what the numbers actually mean. Wireless technology: 2.4 GHz RF, not Bluetooth This is the single most important thing to understand before buying. The AS60 transmits over 2.4 GHz radio frequency from a dedicated base station, not over Bluetooth. That matters for two reasons. First, RF transmission has far less latency than Bluetooth, so lip-sync stays tight. Second, the base station is the audio source, meaning the headphones don't need to pair with anything. You plug the base into your TV's audio output, power it on, and the headphones connect automatically. No pairing menus. No reconnecting every time you turn them on. Compared to similar TV headphones. This is the category standard. Most products designed specifically for TV listening use RF rather than Bluetooth for exactly these reasons. Where the AS60 differentiates is range: 197 feet is well above average. Most RF TV headphones claim somewhere between 100 and 150 feet* (based on available data). In practice, 197 feet means you can walk to the kitchen, the garage, or upstairs and keep hearing the broadcast. Walls and appliances will cut that number down, but even at half the rated distance, this covers most homes. The spec sheet also lists [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) and a Bluetooth range of 10 meters. For everyday listening. This is confusing, and the data doesn't fully explain it. The product is marketed as a 2.4 GHz RF device, and the instructions repeatedly describe the base station as the connection point. It's possible Bluetooth is listed as a spec for the chipset rather than as an active attribute. If you specifically want Bluetooth headphones to pair with your phone, don't assume this product does that based on the spec line. The wired mode is the confirmed alternative. Compatibility: broad, with notable exceptions The AS60 handles optical, RCA, and 3.5mm audio outputs. That covers a huge share of TVs made in the last fifteen years. It does not support HDMI audio out. Which is a real limitation. If your TV only has HDMI ARC or eARC for audio output and no optical port. This product won't work for you. Many newer budget TVs have dropped optical output entirely. So check your TV's ports before ordering. There's also a catch with optical connections. If you use the optical out, you must set your TV's audio format to PCM. Dolby and DTS aren't supported. This is a common limitation in this price range, and it's a dealbreaker for some people. If your TV defaults to Dolby Digital and doesn't offer a PCM option in the audio menu, the headphones may produce silence or static. The listing is upfront about this, which is worth noting, but it's the kind of detail that trips people up after purchase. The RCA compatibility has an additional caveat: it doesn't work with RCA outputs on AV receivers or amplifiers. So if you were hoping to run this off your home theater receiver's RCA out, that won't work. In real-world use, it's designed for TV audio outputs, not receiver outputs. One more thing the listing acknowledges: when the transmitter is plugged in. Your TV's internal speakers may mute or stop working. Whether that happens depends on your TV model. For some households, that's fine - the whole point is private listening. For others. It means everyone else loses the TV audio when one person puts on headphones. There's no workaround mentioned in the data. Audio quality: DSP modes are the main story The AS60 uses a digital signal processor with three selectable modes: Musical, Movie, and Normal. The Movie mode is described as adding "shock bass and 3D surround." This is a common feature in TV headphones, and the practical effect is usually a wider, more cinematic soundstage with boosted low end. Whether it sounds solid is subjective, but the presence of a dedicated DSP chip is a positive sign compared to budget headphones that just pass the signal through. The driver type is listed as bone conduction. Which is almost certainly a data error. Bone conduction headphones sit on your cheekbones and don't cover your ears. The AS60 is described as over-ear with memory foam earpads that seal audio in. That's a conventional dynamic driver design, not bone conduction. Treat the bone conduction line as noise. The frequency range of 20Hz to 20. 000Hz is standard for consumer headphones and tells you nothing unusual. The impedance figure is listed as 10 ohm. With a sensitivity of 14.2 ohm. Sensitivity is normally measured in decibels, not ohms, so that spec is likely mislabeled or incorrect. The 10-ohm impedance is very low, which means the headphones are easy to drive. That's fine for a self-powered wireless headset, but it also means these aren't designed for high-fidelity amplification. The listing itself says they're not for professional audiophiles, and the specs support that. This is a convenience product, not a essential listening tool. Battery life and charging: solid. With a caveat Ten hours of playback per charge, with a three-hour recharge time, is above average for TV headphones. In everyday use, many competitors in this category claim six to eight hours. Ten hours covers multiple movie nights or a full day of intermittent TV watching before you need to dock them. For most people, the charging base doubles as the transmitter, which is a smart design. You set the base near your TV, plug it into wall power, and the headphones charge whenever you set them down. No separate charging cable to manage. One unusual instruction: use the included USB adapter to plug the base into a wall outlet. Not a USB port on another device. The listing warns against using a TV's USB port or a computer's USB port for power. This suggests the base needs more current than a standard USB port provides, or that the manufacturer has seen reliability issues when people power it from low-amperage sources. Either way. Follow the instruction. It's a minor inconvenience if your TV unboxing doesn't have an open wall outlet nearby, but most people can manage.
