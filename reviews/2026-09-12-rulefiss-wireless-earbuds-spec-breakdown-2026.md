# Rulefiss Wireless Earbuds: Spec Breakdown (2026)

what I appreciate the Rulefiss Q38 is a sports earbud built around a specific set of priorities: staying in your ears during movement. Surviving sweat, and running long enough between charges that you stop thinking about the battery. On those three counts, the spec sheet is genuinely competitive. Where it's weaker is the stuff you'd notice during desk work or critical listening - the noise cancellation is call-focused rather than music-focused, and there's no [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) for blocking out a gym or a train. If you want earbuds for running. Lifting, or cycling, the Q38's design choices line up with what you actually need. If you want a do-everything pair for commuting and office focus, the priorities are pointed in the wrong direction. The earhook is the defining aspect. And it's worth understanding what that buys you. A 45° flexible hook that wraps over the top of your ear is the same entry-level approach used by most purpose-built sport earbuds. And it solves the single biggest complaint people have with standard in-ears during exercise: they work loose when you sweat or move your jaw. An 8-gram earbud is light enough that the hook doesn't need to clamp hard to stay put. Which matters because a hook that squeezes too tightly becomes painful after 30 minutes* (based on available data). Three sizes of silicone tips (S/M/L) are included, and getting the right tip seal is what actually determines bass response and stability - the hook keeps the bud from falling out, but the tip seal keeps it from sounding thin. If you've had trouble with earbuds popping out mid-run. This design addresses that directly. If you've never had that problem. The hook is mostly extra bulk. The IP-X7 rating is the other spec that matters most for the intended use. IP-X7 means the earbuds can survive immersion in water up to a meter for 30 minutes* (based on available data), which is well beyond what sweat or rain requires. In practice, that means you can rinse them under a tap after a workout, wear them in heavy rain, and not worry about sweat pooling in the crevices. Most earbuds in this category stop at [IPX4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) or [IPX5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code), which handles splashes but not submersion. The extra headroom is real, not marketing. One caveat: the rating applies to the earbuds. Not the charging case. Don't dunk the case. Battery life is where the numbers need some translation. Rulefiss claims 6-8 hours per charge and up to 60 hours total with the case. The technical specifications list "battery average life: 6 hours," so treat 6 as the realistic single-charge figure and 8 as the best case with volume low and [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) off (there's no [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) to turn off, so that variable is moot). Six hours covers a marathon, a long ride, or several gym sessions before you need the case. The 800mAh case adds roughly nine or ten full recharges, which is where the 60-hour total comes from. That's a large number. But it's worth noting that the case is doing the heavy lifting - the earbuds themselves aren't unusually long-lasting. For everyday listening, the LED display showing percentage from 1% to 100% is a compact thing that makes a real difference. Most budget cases show four dots or a blinking light, which tells you almost nothing. A percentage readout means you know whether you have enough for tomorrow's run before you leave the house. The 1.5-hour charge time for the case is quick, and [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) is the right port to have. The headphone jack is listed as [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) "for charging, no audio," which is worth being explicit about: there's no wired listening option at all. If the battery dies mid-flight. You can't plug in a cable and keep listening. That's a limitation, though a common one at this tier. Sound quality claims deserve scrutiny. The 14.2mm composite diaphragm is a large driver for an in-ear - most budget earbuds use 6mm to 10mm drivers. A larger driver generally moves more air and can produce deeper bass without distorting, which is consistent with the "3D bass" positioning. The frequency range is listed inconsistently: the aspect copy says 18Hz-25kHz. But the technical specifications say 18Hz-20kHz. The lower figure is almost certainly the accurate one, since 20kHz is the standard upper limit of human hearing and 25kHz is a marketing stretch. Either way. The 18Hz low end is optimistic for any earbud - you'll feel bass, but the deepest sub-bass frequencies are more felt than heard on any in-ear. The 32-ohm impedance and 108dB sensitivity mean these will get loud easily from a phone without needing a headphone amp. That's a practical advantage: you won't max out the volume and still find it quiet. The AAC/SBC codec support tells you something important. AAC is the codec used by iPhones and most streaming services. And SBC is the universal fallback. There's no [aptX](https://www.aptx.com/) or [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) here, so Android users won't get the higher-bitrate codecs some competing earbuds offer. For gym use and casual listening. AAC and SBC are fine - you won't notice the difference over the sound of a treadmill. For essential listening, you would. The 41ms latency figure in the characteristic copy versus 31ms in the technical specs is another inconsistency; either number is low enough for video and casual gaming, though the listing also says the earbuds are "not compatible with gaming devices," which is a strange caveat. Bluetooth audio works with anything that works with Bluetooth audio, so treat that note as noise rather than a real restriction. [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) is current-generation, and the claimed improvements over 5.3 - faster transmission, better stability, enhanced anti-interference - are the kind of incremental gains that matter more in crowded RF environments like a gym full of people on their own earbuds. The 20-meter range is roughly double the typical 10-meter Bluetooth range, which means you can leave your phone on a bench and walk to the other side of the weight room without dropouts. That's a genuinely useful spec for the intended use. Dual-channel transmission means each earbud connects independently rather than one relaying to the other. Which reduces the chance of one bud cutting out when you turn your head. Single/twin mode is supported, so you can use just one earbud if you want to keep an ear open. Call quality is handled by four microphones and ENC (Environmental Noise Cancellation). ENC isn't the same as [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). ENC processes the microphone signal to isolate your voice from background noise on calls; it does nothing for what you hear. [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) actively cancels ambient noise in your ears so music sounds cleaner. The Q38 has ENC but no [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). That means your callers will hear less of the gym around you. But you'll still hear all of it. For running outdoors, that's safer - you want to hear traffic. For noisy commutes or open-plan offices, it means you'll be turning the volume up to drown out the environment, which is worse for your hearing and drains the battery faster. Be clear on this distinction before buying: the "[noise cancelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)" in the title refers to call clarity, not listening isolation. In real-world use, the touch controls cover the standard functions: play, pause, volume, track skip, calls, and voice assistant activation. Touch panels on budget earbuds can be finicky - accidental triggers when adjusting the fit are common, and the glossy coating mentioned in the listing tends to show fingerprints and get slippery when your hands are sweaty. That's a minor cosmetic issue, but worth knowing if you care about how they look after a few months. The ABS plastic enclosure is standard for this price tier; it's rugged enough but won't feel premium. Compatibility is broad: iOS. Android, phones, tablets, laptops, smart watches, and TVs. The automatic reconnection when you remove them from the case is the expected behavior nowadays. And one-step pairing is standard. For most people, the listing notes the manual is available in English, Spanish, French, and other languages, which is a small but useful detail if English isn't your first language.
