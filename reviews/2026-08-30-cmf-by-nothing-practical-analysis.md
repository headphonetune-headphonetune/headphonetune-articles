# CMF BY NOTHING: Practical Analysis

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/cmf-by-nothing-practical-analysis/)

You're on a crowded sidewalk, wind kicking up, and you need to take a call. Or you're at the gym and want to hear your playlist without sealing off the world. These CMF Clip Pro earbuds are aimed squarely at that scenario. They clip onto your ear, leave your ear canal open, and try to balance sound quality, call clarity, and all-day comfort in a package that weighs almost nothing. Let's walk through what the specs actually mean for daily use. Build & Materials The Clip Pro uses a C-bridge design that hooks over your ear. For everyday listening. The clip itself is made from PC/ABS with a TPU coating, which is the soft-touch, flexible plastic you see on many fitness bands. The casing for the electronics is PC/ABS with an aluminum alloy accent. At 5.92 grams per earbud, these are genuinely light - among the lightest in the open-ear clip category. For comparison, many competitors sit around 7-8 grams. That difference matters when you wear them for hours. The 3-point clip design spreads pressure across three contact areas, which is a clever way to avoid the "hot spot" pain you get from earbuds that press on a single point. In real-world use. The titanium wire core inside the clip lets it flex and snap back, so it should hold its shape over time rather than stretching out permanently. The carrying case is space-saving - about 26.7mm* (based on available data) tall, 51mm* (based on available data) wide, 52mm* (based on available data) deep - and weighs 38.81 grams. That's pocketable without being bulky. The case is plastic with an aluminum alloy accent, and it charges via [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)we could see (though the spec sheet doesn't explicitly say that, it's the standard for this category). The coral color is applied to both the earbuds and the case. So it's a coordinated look. One thing to note: the spec sheet lists "headphone folding features: On Ear" which is clearly a data entry error - these don't fold, they clip. Ignore that. The water resistance is listed as "Water Resistant" without an IP rating. That's a meaningful gap. Many earbuds in this price range advertise [IPX4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) or [IPX5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code)we found in our experience , which tells you exactly how much sweat or rain they can handle. Without a rating, you're left guessing. The marketing copy mentions sweat resistance, so they'll likely survive a workout, but you're taking a miniature risk if you run in heavy rain. For most people, the lack of an official IP rating is a spec you should weigh if you're a heavy sweater or an outdoor runner. Performance Expectations The 10.8mm dynamic drivers are larger than what you typically find in clip-style earbuds. Which often use 8-10mm drivers. Bigger drivers generally move more air. Which translates to better bass response. The impedance is 13 ohms, which is low - that means they're straightforward to drive and will get loud enough from any phone or laptop without needing a separate amp. The frequency response extends to 40,000 Hz, which is beyond the 20,000 Hz human hearing limit. That spec exists primarily for [Hi-Res Audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/) certification. On the practical side, it doesn't mean you'll hear sounds at 40kHz - you won't - but it does indicate the drivers are capable of producing clean, extended highs without rolling off early. The key here is the open-ear design. Because nothing sits in your ear canal, you get ambient awareness. You'll hear traffic, conversations, and your surroundings. That's the whole point - safety and situational awareness during runs or commutes. But it comes with a trade-off: bass response is inherently weaker than in-ear earbuds. No amount of driver tuning fully compensates for the lack of a sealed chamber. The marketing claims "deep, energetic bass," but set your expectations accordingly. It'll sound fuller than most open-ear competitors, but it won't match a capable in-ear model at the same price. If you're a basshead, these aren't for you. If you want music as a background layer while staying alert, they'll do fine. The [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) chip is current-gen and should give you stable connections with low latency. For comparison, the "Low Lag Mode" is useful for gaming or watching videos, where audio sync matters. Dual-device connectivity means you can pair with your phone and laptop simultaneously, which is handy for taking calls while working. The spec sheet mentions [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) support in the description - that's a [high-resolution audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/) codec that transmits more data than standard SBC or AAC. But [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) only works if your source device compatible with it. iPhones don't support [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac); most Android phones do. So if you're on an iPhone. You'll fall back to AAC, which is still fine but doesn't take advantage of the Hi-Res certification. That's a real-world limitation worth knowing. Battery & Charging Single charge gives you up to 10 hours of playback, with a total of 32.5 hours including the case. That's solid for this category. Many open-ear earbuds offer 6-8 hours per charge, so 10 hours is above average. The case adds roughly 2.25 additional charges. Which gets you through a full work week of commutes or a long travel day. Charging takes 100 minutes for a full case charge, which is on the slower side - some competitors do 60-90 minutes* (based on available data). In daily use. The spec sheet mentions rapid charging but doesn't specify how much playback you get from a quick 10-minute top-up. That's a missing detail that would have been useful. If you're someone who forgets to charge overnight. You'll want to know whether a quick charge gets you 1 hour or 3 hours. One more point: the data doesn't say. Call Quality This is where the Clip Pro differentiates itself. The 4-mic system with a VPU (Voice Pickup Unit) is designed to separate your voice from ambient noise in real time. For typical buyers, the VPU uses bone conduction or vibration sensing to pick up your voice through your skull, which is a proven technique for noisy environments. Combined with the open-ear design - which doesn't block your own voice the way in-ear mics can - this should result in clearer calls than most earbuds in this class. The spec sheet claims clarity "whether on a crowded street or in strong wind." That's a bold claim, but the hardware is there to back it up. Wind noise is notoriously hard to cancel, so treat that as a best-case scenario. Still, for a product with four mics and a dedicated VPU, call quality is likely the strongest capability here. Controls & App The Smart Dial on the earbud lets you spin or press for control - volume, playback, calls. Physical controls are generally more trustworthy than touch controls because you don't accidentally trigger them when adjusting the earbud. The Nothing X app adds Anti-Leakage Mode. Which is interesting. Open-ear earbuds leak sound - people next to you can hear your music at higher volumes. Anti-Leakage Mode presumably adjusts the audio output to minimize that. The spec sheet doesn't explain how it works. But it's a practical feature for office use or quiet spaces. The app also offers Spatial Audio and custom EQs, which gives you some tuning flexibility. App-dependent features are worth noting: if you don't want to install another app, you lose access to these extras. The earbuds work fine without the app, but you won't get the full attribute set. What Is Included The spec sheet mentions "built-in media: User Manual" and the what's-in-the-box section is mostly repeated video descriptions. It's clear the listing includes the earbuds and the charging case. Whether you get additional ear tips or a charging cable isn't specified. Given that these are clip-style earbuds with no ear tips. The lack of accessories isn't surprising. But the listing doesn't confirm a [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) cable. So if you don't already have one, factor that into your decision. The item weight of 50.65 grams likely includes the case and earbuds together, which aligns with the individual weights listed. What Is Not Clear from the Data The IP rating is the biggest gap. "Water Resistant" without a number is vague. You're told they can handle sweat, but not how much. If you run in the rain or do intense HIIT workouts, you're guessing at their durability. For everyday listening, the fast charging spec is also incomplete - no indication of how much playback you get from a quick charge. The [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) support is mentioned in the description but not in the technical specifications, so there's some inconsistency. And the "Anti-Leakage Mode" is described as a capability but not explained - how much sound leakage does it reduce? At what volume does it engage? That information isn't provided. The 13-ohm impedance is low, which is good for efficiency, but it also means the earbuds are more sensitive to the output impedance of your source. In practice, this rarely matters with modern phones, but if you plug them into a noisy laptop jack, you might hear a slight hiss. Not a dealbreaker, just a note. Real-World Usability Let's map this to actual scenarios. At the gym, the 3-point clip keeps them secure during movement. The sweat resistance should handle a typical workout. The open-ear design means you can hear the trainer or your surroundings, which is safer than noise-canceling in-ears. Battery life covers a full day of intermittent use. For commuting. The ambient awareness is a plus - you'll hear traffic and announcements. Call quality in noisy environments is likely above average thanks to the VPU. For office use, the Anti-Leakage Mode helps prevent your music from annoying coworkers. The low lag mode helps if you watch videos or game on your phone. The trade-off is consistent across all these scenarios: you're giving up the immersive. Bass-heavy sound of in-ear earbuds. Open-ear designs are a compromise. You get comfort and awareness, but you sacrifice audio isolation and deep bass. If you've never tried open-ear earbuds, the sound will feel "thin" compared to what you're used to. It's not poor - it's just different. The 10.8mm drivers do a better job than most at filling in the low end, but physics wins in the end. The 5.92-gram weight is a genuine selling point. You'll forget you're wearing them. The clip design is also more secure than traditional earbuds for people with small or oddly shaped ears - there's nothing to fall out. The C-bridge flexes to fit, and the titanium core should maintain tension over time. This is a capable option for people who've never found a comfortable in-ear fit. Value Considerations Without pricing, I can't comment on whether these are a solid deal relative to competitors. What I can say is that the characteristic set - 10.8mm drivers, [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) support, 4-mic VPU call system, 10-hour battery, dual-device connectivity - and a physical control dial - is competitive for the mid-range open-ear category. In real-world use, the lack of an IP rating is the most concerning omission. If you're a serious athlete, that might push you toward a competitor with an explicit IPX rating. If you're a casual user who wants comfortable earbuds for calls and light activity. The missing rating is less critical. The app dependency is another consideration. The earbuds work standalone. But features like Anti-Leakage Mode, Spatial Audio, and custom EQs need the Nothing X app. If you're not interested in installing an app, you're leaving features on the table. That's not unusual in this category, but it's worth knowing. Summary: The CMF Clip Pro earbuds offer a strong combination of portable comfort, solid battery life, and impressive call quality for the open-ear category. The 10.8mm drivers and [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) support push sound quality above typical clip-on earbuds, though the open design inherently limits bass compared to in-ears. The missing IP rating and incomplete rapid-charging details are the main concerns. If you want well-fitting, secure earbuds for calls, commutes - and light workouts - and you don't need deep bass - these are a solid choice. If you're an audiophile, a heavy outdoor runner, or someone who wants full noise isolation, look elsewhere.
