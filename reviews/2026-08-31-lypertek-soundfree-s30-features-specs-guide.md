# LYPERTEK SoundFree S30: Features &amp; Specs Guide

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/lypertek-soundfree-s30-features-specs-guide/)

I think our analysis shows you're at the pool deck, goggles on, ready to knock out a mile. The last thing you want is your phone in a Ziploc bag at the edge of the lane, or worse, in your pocket while you swim. This is the exact scenario the LYPERTEK SoundFree S30 is built for. It's a bone conduction headphone with a 32GB MP3 player and [IP68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code)based on our review waterproofing, which is a combination you don't see every day. Let's break down what that actually means for you, and where it falls short. The Underwater Playback Situation The headline attribute here's the 32GB of internal storage. Compared to similar products in the swimming headphone category, this is a significant step up. Many competitors in this price range offer 8GB or 16GB* (based on available data), which is fine for a few playlists but forces you to rotate your library. The spec sheet claims 8,000+ songs, which is a marketing number based on average file sizes, but the storage itself is genuinely useful. You can load lossless files or a solid podcast backlog and not think about it for months. This is the core : you leave your phone in the locker. Bluetooth doesn't travel through water well, so any swim-focused headphone requires this offline capability to be relevant. For everyday listening, the S30 has it, and it has it in spades. The Bluetooth 6.0 chip is for land use. That's a current-generation spec, and it's above average for this category. Most swim headphones I've seen are still on [Bluetooth 5.3](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) or 5.4. The 10-meter range is standard-don't expect to wander across the gym and keep a signal. The dual-mic ENC for calls is a fine touch, but bone conduction mics are never great in noisy environments. In real-world use, it'll be fine for a quick call on a walk, but don't expect conference-call quality. Waterproofing: The Real Deal or Marketing Fluff? The [IP68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code)our analysis shows rating with TÜV Rheinland 10m certification is the spec that matters most for a swimmer. [IP68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) is the highest ingress protection rating, meaning it's dust-tight and can be submerged beyond 1 meter. The TÜV certification for 10 meters is a third-party verification, which is more credible than a self-reported spec. Compared to similar products, this is above average. Many budget swim headphones claim [IP68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) but lack the independent certification. The practical takeaway: you can wear these for pool laps, open water swims, and even snorkeling at shallow depths without worry. The 10m depth rating is generous; you're not going to hit that in a pool. One caveat: the listing mentions the [IP68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) rating but doesn't specify how long the waterproofing lasts. Waterproof seals degrade over time, especially with chlorine exposure. The 1-year warranty covers defects, but not normal wear. That's standard for the category, but worth noting-this is a consumable item if you swim daily. Bone Conduction: The Trade-Off You Need to Accept The S30 uses bone conduction, which means it vibrates your cheekbones to transmit sound, leaving your ear canals open. This is the right choice for swimming because it lets you hear ambient sounds (like a lifeguard's whistle) and prevents the "water in the ear" muffled effect you get with in-ear buds. The 23g weight with a titanium memory-wire frame is a key spec. Compared to similar products, this is light. Many swim headphones weigh 30-35g* (based on available data). For most people, the titanium frame is a durability plus-it should hold its shape after being tossed in a gym bag. But here's the honest trade-off: sound quality. The frequency range is listed as 20Hz-20kHz, which is the standard human hearing range, but that's a spec that means nothing in practice. Bone conduction drivers are physically limited. They lack bass response, and at high volumes, they can vibrate uncomfortably. The 50-ohm impedance is on the higher side, which usually means it demands more power to drive, but with a built-in MP3 player, that's not an issue. On the practical side, the three EQ modes are a nod to this limitation-you'll likely want to switch to a "bass boost" mode to get any low-end presence. Compared to a standard pair of in-ear sport earbuds, the S30 will sound thin. That's not a flaw in this product; it's physics. You're buying situational awareness and underwater capability, not audiophile fidelity. Fit and Comfort for Training The open-ear design with a titanium memory wire is a proven formula for sports headphones. The 23g weight is low enough that you should forget you're wearing it after a few minutes. For comparison, the soft silicone coating is standard for this category, but it does help prevent chafing during long sessions. The "secure fit" claim is plausible based on the materials, but there's no data on how well it holds up during flip turns. If you're a competitive swimmer doing aggressive turns, the open-ear band might shift. That's a risk with any bone conduction headphone-they don't anchor in the ear canal, so they rely on the frame's grip. The listing doesn't mention any included earplugs or fins, which some competitors offer to enhance bass underwater. That's a minor miss. In daily use, the rear safety LED is a practical addition for early morning or night runs. It's not a distinctive feature, but it's above average for this price point. Many swim headphones skip this entirely. It's a small thing, but if you're cycling or running in low light, it adds a layer of visibility. Charging and Connectivity Magnetic charging is the right call for a waterproof device. It eliminates the flimsy rubber flap that eventually breaks on micro-USB ports. For typical buyers, the spec sheet doesn't list battery life, which is a notable omission. For a swimming headphone, you'd want at least 8 hours of playback to cover a week of training. Without that data, you're taking a risk. The magnetic cable is proprietary, so if you lose it, you'll need to buy a replacement from the manufacturer. That's a common annoyance in this category. The Bluetooth 6.0 is forward-looking, but it's only relevant for land-based listening. Underwater, you'll use the MP3 player. The ENC (Environmental Noise Cancellation) on the mics is a standard aspect now, but don't expect it to work miracles. Bone conduction mics pick up wind and vibration, so calls on a windy road will still sound rough. What's Missing or Vague The spec sheet is quiet on battery life, as I noted. It's also quiet on charging time. For a product aimed at daily training, that's a gap. You're left guessing whether it'll last a week of 1-hour swims or die after two sessions. The listing also doesn't specify the exact Bluetooth profile support (AAC, [aptX](https://www.aptx.com/), etc.), which matters if you're an Android user who cares about codec quality. For swimming, it's irrelevant, but for gym use, it's a consideration. The controls are listed as "Push Button, Touch." That's a hybrid approach. Touch controls on a wet device can be finicky-water droplets can trigger accidental inputs. Physical buttons are more solid underwater. For everyday listening, the fact that it has both suggests you can use the buttons for swim mode and touch for land. That's a smart design choice, but the implementation is unverified. The Shokz OpenSwim has 4GB of storage and no Bluetooth, and it costs more. In real-world use, the Naenka Runner Diver has 16GB* (based on available data) and Bluetooth, at a similar price to the S30. Compared to those, the LYPERTEK S30 offers double the storage of the Naenka and adds Bluetooth, which the Shokz lacks. That's a strong on paper. The [IP68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) rating with TÜV certification matches or exceeds both competitors. The 23g weight is lighter than the Naenka (which is around 30g). So, on specs alone, this product is above average for its class, especially at this price point. For most people, the 4.1-star rating from 40 reviews is a space-saving sample size, so treat it with caution. It's not a red flag, but it's not a proven track record either. The best seller rank (#884 in Open-Ear Headphones) suggests it's not a top-tier seller, but it's not invisible. The Verdict on Real-World Usability Here's the practical breakdown. For a pool swimmer who wants to ditch the phone, the S30 is a compelling option. The 32GB storage is genuinely liberating-you can load your entire library and not manage it weekly. On the practical side, the [IP68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) rating gives you peace of mind. The open-ear design is safe for a pool environment. The weight is low enough for long sessions. For a gym-goer who wants a do-everything headphone, the S30 is less perfect. Bone conduction sound quality is inferior to in-ear buds at the same price. The lack of battery life data is a risk if you're doing multi-hour sessions. For comparison, the touch controls might be annoying with sweaty hands. For a runner, the S30 works, but the bone conduction sound will be thinner than a traditional pair of sport earbuds. The safety LED is a plus, but the open-ear design lets in wind noise, which can mask the audio. The missing battery life spec is the biggest red flag. It's a fundamental piece of information for a portable device. If you're considering this, you should look for independent reviews that measure actual playback time. In daily use, the 50-ohm impedance is also slightly unusual-most consumer headphones are 32 ohms or lower-but with the built-in player, it's not a practical concern. Summary: The LYPERTEK SoundFree S30 is a purpose-built swimming headphone that offers above-average storage (32GB) and verified [IP68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) waterproofing (TÜV 10m certification) compared to its direct competitors. The bone conduction design is appropriate for water use, and the 23g weight is well-fitting. However, the lack of battery life data is a significant gap in the spec sheet, and the sound quality will be thin compared to traditional earbuds. If you swim regularly and want to go phone-free, this is a strong candidate. If you need a flexible headphone for all-day use or prioritize audio fidelity, look elsewhere. Who this is for: The lap swimmer who trains multiple times a week and wants a dependable, phone-free audio workaround. The triathlete who needs one device for the pool and the open water. The runner who trains in low light and values the safety LED and open-ear awareness. Anyone who prioritizes waterproofing and storage capacity over sound quality. Who should look elsewhere: The gym-goer who wants a single headphone for lifting, cardio, and commuting-bone conduction won't satisfy your bass requires. The audiophile who listens to lossless files and demands detail-this isn't that. For typical buyers, the budget-conscious buyer who doesn't swim-you can get better-sounding Bluetooth earbuds for less money. Anyone who needs a headphone for long-distance cycling or running where wind noise is a factor-the open-ear design will let in too much ambient sound.
