# IMYB A36 Gaming &#8211; Headphones Review (2026)

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/imyb-a36-gaming-headphones-review-2026/)

The IMYB A36 is a wired. Over-ear gaming headset that sells in huge volumes - it's ranked #8 in PC Game Headsets online - and carries a 4.3-star average from over 3,800 reviews. That's a lot of buyers, so the real question is whether the specs explain why, or whether people are just tolerating it because it's economical. Here's the breakdown. Sound quality: above average for the price, but not what "subwoofer" implies The headline spec here is the 50mm neodymium driver. Compared to similar products at this price point. Most gaming headsets use 40mm* (based on available data) drivers. That 10mm difference is real - larger drivers move more air, which typically translates to stronger bass presence and a wider soundstage. For everyday listening. The listing calls this a "Surrounding Stereo Subwoofer," which is marketing nonsense; there's no actual subwoofer in a headphone. What you're getting is a driver that can reproduce low frequencies with more authority than a 40mm* (based on available data) unit. The frequency response is listed as 20-20,000 Hz, which is the standard audible range for human hearing. That's not impressive on paper - almost every headphone on the market claims this - but it's also not a red flag. The sensitivity is 42 dB. Which is on the lower side. Compared to similar products that often hit 95-105 dB* (based on available data), this figure looks odd. If that spec is accurate, you'll need to push the volume higher on your source device to get the same loudness. In practice, that means on a phone or laptop you may find yourself at 80-90% volume where another headset would sit at 60%. It's not a dealbreaker. But it's below what is expected at this price point. The 32-ohm impedance is friendly to most devices. You can plug this into a phone. Tablet, or laptop without needing an external amplifier. That's standard for the category and means the headset will work with anything that has a 3.5mm jack. Build and comfort: light. But the materials raise questions At 0.77 lb (about 350 grams* (based on available data)), this headset is lighter than many competitors. Typical gaming headsets from brands like HyperX or Razer often weigh between 300 and 400 grams* (based on available data). So this sits comfortably in the middle. For long gaming sessions. Lighter is generally better - neck fatigue is a real issue with heavy headsets, and this one shouldn't cause problems. The ear cushions are described as "skin-friendly" faux leather, and the headband has "thickening pads." The enclosure is ABS plastic with faux leather covering. Compared to similar products at this price, that's standard. What you should know is that the faux leather tends to peel after a year or two of regular use, especially in warmer climates where sweat degrades the material faster. In real-world use, the 3-year warranty covers hardware failures, but cosmetic peeling may not qualify. One thing the data doesn't tell you is clamping force. The specs don't mention how tightly the headband grips your head. And that's a critical comfort factor. Some users report that budget headsets squeeze too hard. Causing discomfort after A hour. With no data, I can't confirm or deny that here. What I can say is that the weight is manageable, and the over-ear oval shape is the most common and generally well-fitting design. Microphone: functional. But don't expect broadcast quality The mic is described as omnidirectional with "noise isolating" function. That's a contradiction in terms - an omnidirectional mic picks up sound from all directions. Which means it captures background noise. True noise isolation requires a directional (cardioid) pickup pattern. What the listing likely means is that the mic has some noise reduction processing, but the physical design is omnidirectional. Compared to similar products, this is a budget compromise. Most dedicated gaming headsets in the mid-range use cardioid mics to reject keyboard clatter and room noise. For most people. This one will pick up your voice clearly in a quiet room, but if you have a mechanical keyboard or loud fans, your teammates will hear them. The mic is integrated into the headset, not detachable. That's worth noting - if the mic breaks. You can't just remove it and use the headphones for music. The listing doesn't specify whether the mic boom is flexible or fixed. So I can't speak to adjustability. There's a mute button on the inline control, which is standard. Connectivity: the real story here's the adapter situation This is where you need to pay attention. On the practical side, the IMYB A36 uses a 3.5mm jack. It's compatible with PC, PS4, PS5, Nintendo Switch, laptops, tablets, and phones - anything with a 3.5mm port. That's the easy part. For Xbox One and Xbox Series X/S, there's a catch. The listing says "older version requires adapter." This is a common issue with third-party headsets. Modern Xbox controllers have a 3.5mm jack built in. So this headset will work if you plug directly into the controller. But if you have an older Xbox One controller without the 3.5mm port, you'll need a separate adapter (the Xbox Stereo Headset Adapter) that plugs into the controller's proprietary port. That adapter isn't included. The box comes with the headset, a user manual, and a 1-to-2 3.5mm splitter cable - no Xbox adapter. If you're on an older Xbox controller. Factor in that extra purchase. The splitter cable is for PC users with separate headphone and mic jacks. Most modern laptops have a single combo jack, but older desktops and some gaming laptops still use two separate ports. Including the splitter is a thoughtful touch and saves you a trip to buy one separately. Compared to similar products that often skip this, it's above average for the class. Wired vs. wireless: know what you're giving up This is a wired headset. The cable is 6.5 feet of braided, tangle-resistant wire. That's a decent length - long enough to reach from your PC tower to your chair without pulling. Braided cables are more durable than rubber ones and resist tangling better. Compared to similar products, this is standard-to-good for the price. But wired means no battery, no charging, and no wireless latency. If you're playing competitive shooters. Wired is actually preferable because it eliminates wireless interference and battery anxiety. If you're used to wireless freedom - getting up to grab a drink without removing the headset - this will feel restrictive. The 6.5-foot cable gives you some slack. But you're still tethered to your device. Controls: physical and solid Volume control is a rotary knob on the cable, and the mic mute is a button. This is the right choice for a budget headset. Rotary knobs are mechanical and rarely fail; they give you precise control without hunting for buttons. Inline controls on the cable are easier to find by touch than controls on the earcup. Compared to similar products that sometimes put touch controls on the earcup (which you accidentally trigger when adjusting the headset), this is a practical design. Nothing fancy, but it works. Noise cancellation: passive only, and that's fine The listing says "Passive Noise Cancellation." That's just the physical isolation from the ear cushions - the faux leather pads create a seal around your ears that blocks some ambient noise. There's no [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) ([ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)) circuitry here. At this price point, that's expected. You won't get the near-silence of [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headphones. But for gaming at home, passive isolation is usually enough. The 50mm drivers and over-ear seal will block out low-level household noise like a TV in the next room, but not loud conversations or traffic. If you need to game in a noisy environment - a shared living room or a coffee shop - this won't save you. Look for [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headsets if that's your situation. But expect to pay A key detail: more. What's missing from the spec sheet There's no information on whether the headband is adjustable beyond the "flexible" description. Most headsets at this price have a sliding adjustment mechanism. But I can't confirm it here. If you have a very large or very petite head, this is a gamble. There's no mention of ear cushion replaceability. Once the faux leather wears out, you may not be able to buy replacements. That limits the lifespan to however long the cushions last - typically 1-2 years with daily use. The warranty is 3 years limited hardware, which is better than the typical 1-year warranty on budget headsets. That's a point in its favor, though "limited" usually means it covers manufacturing defects, not wear and tear. Category comparison: where does this land? The IMYB A36 exceeds that baseline with 50mm drivers. A braided cable. And a splitter included. For comparison, the 42 dB sensitivity is the weak point - most competitors spec higher, which means the A36 may require more volume from your source. That's a trade-off: you get bigger drivers and deeper bass, but you may need to crank the volume to hear details at low levels. The multi-platform compatibility is genuinely broad. PC, PS4, PS5, Switch, mobile - all work with a single 3.5mm plug. The Xbox caveat (older controllers need an adapter) is worth remembering, but modern Xbox controllers work fine. The 3. 857 reviews with a 4.3 average suggest that most buyers are satisfied. That's a solid signal. But review averages on budget electronics tend to skew positive because the expectations are low - people compare it to other headsets. Not to premium audio gear. The question isn't whether this is better than a headset; it's whether it does the job without frustration. Based on the specs, it should. Summary: The IMYB A36 is a solid budget gaming headset that gets the fundamentals right: large 50mm drivers for bass-heavy game audio. A light build at 0.77 lb, broad device compatibility, and a braided cable with practical inline controls. The 42 dB sensitivity is below average and may require higher volume levels. The omnidirectional mic won't reject background noise well, and the faux leather cushions will wear over time. In daily use, it's a capable choice for PC, PlayStation, or Switch gamers who want a wired headset that works without fuss and don't need wireless freedom or [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). Who this is for: PC and PlayStation gamers who want a cheap. Wired headset with decent bass response and a comfortable weight for long sessions. If you play on a modern Xbox controller with a 3.5mm jack, it works there too. Anyone who needs a headset that plugs into multiple devices - laptop, phone, Switch - without buying adapters will appreciate the simple 3.5mm connection. Budget-conscious buyers who prioritize function over top-tier materials will find this adequate. Who should look elsewhere: Xbox owners with older controllers that lack a 3.5mm jack - you'll need to buy a separate adapter, which adds cost. Gamers in noisy environments who need real noise isolation should look for a headset with a directional mic and tighter ear seal, or step up to [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) models. If you're sensitive to low volume output.
