[ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) and ENC: two different things [Active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)we observed uses microphones to sample ambient sound and generate inverse waves that cancel it out. That's what [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) does here. It's most effective against constant low-frequency noise - engine rumble on a plane. The drone of a bus, air conditioning hum. It does much less against sudden or high-frequency sounds like conversation or a baby crying. How well this particular [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)we found performs is impossible to judge from the spec sheet. For everyday listening, the listing says it "effectively reduces ambient noise" but gives no decibel reduction figure. Budget [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) earbuds typically cut 15 to 25dB* (based on available data). While premium models like Sony or Bose can hit 30dB* (based on available data) or more. Without a measurement. Assume this sits on the lower end. It'll take the edge off, not create silence. ENC is a separate aspect for calls. It filters background noise on your end so the person you're talking to hears your voice more clearly. In real-world use, this is table-stakes for any earbud with call functionality - and its inclusion here is expected, not remarkable. Transparency mode does the opposite of [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) - it pipes outside sound in through the microphones so you can hear traffic. Announcements, or a coworker without removing the buds. Useful for cycling or walking through a city. The spec sheet says it amplifies surrounding sounds in real time. Again. No detail on how natural it sounds. Inexpensive transparency modes often sound processed and echoey. Like you're hearing the world through a paper cup. Without hands-on testing.
