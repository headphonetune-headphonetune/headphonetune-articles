# Philips Audio SHP9500: Features &amp; Specs Guide

Let's set the scene: you're at home. Maybe at a desk - and you want to listen to music without the sound bouncing around inside your skull like a racquetball court. You're not commuting, not trying to block out a snoring partner, and you don't need noise cancellation. You just want a clear, honest reproduction of what's in the recording. That's the territory the Philips SHP9500 lives in. These are open-back. Wired, over-ear headphones with a cult following, and the specs explain why. First, the acoustic design: these are open-back. That's the single most notable thing to understand about this product. The listing states "acoustic system: open" and the description explicitly mentions "open back architecture." In plain terms. The back of each ear cup is vented, not sealed. Air and sound pass through freely. The immediate consequence is that the soundstage - the perceived width and depth of the audio - becomes much larger than a closed-back pair. Instruments don't feel crammed between your ears; they spread out in front of you. Like you're listening to speakers in a room rather than a headphone. That's a genuine advantage for key listening, mixing, or just enjoying orchestral, jazz, or live recordings where spatial cues matter. For everyday listening. The trade-off is absolute: everyone within a few feet will hear exactly what you're hearing. If you're in a quiet office. Your neighbor will know every drum fill. If there's a fan running or traffic outside. You'll hear that too, because open-back offers zero isolation from ambient noise. This isn't a headphone for public transport, shared workspaces, or noisy households. It's for a quiet room where you can sit still and listen. The data doesn't hide this - it's the defining characteristic, and you should treat it as a dealbreaker if your environment isn't quiet. Second, the driver and frequency response. The SHP9500 uses 50mm neodymium dynamic drivers. And the frequency range is listed as 12 Hz to 35,000 Hz. Let's translate that. The human ear typically hears 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, and that range shrinks with age. So the low end goes deeper than most people can perceive, and the high end extends well beyond what anyone can hear. What that actually means in practice is that the headphone isn't straining at the edges of its range. In real-world use, the driver has headroom on both ends, which usually translates to a more relaxed, less harsh treble and a bass that doesn't roll off prematurely. The impedance is 32 ohms, and sensitivity is 101 dB. That's a combination that matters. Low impedance and reasonably high sensitivity mean this headphone is effortless to drive. You don't need a dedicated amplifier. Plug it into a laptop. A phone with a 3.5mm jack, a desktop DAC, or a mixing console, and it will reach a healthy volume without hiss or distortion. For most people. The description confirms this, stating it "will work great with and without headphone amplifiers." That's a real plus for someone who doesn't want to buy extra gear. The trade-off here's more subtle: easy to drive often means less headroom for very dynamic peaks in music. And the bass might not have the visceral thump that a closed-back, high-impedance pair with a dedicated amp can deliver. But for most listeners. 32 ohms is the sweet spot - it's flexible and forgiving. The angled drivers are also worth noting. They're positioned to align with your ear canals. Which helps with imaging - pinpointing where instruments sit in the stereo field. That's a thoughtful engineering choice, not just a marketing bullet. Third. The physical design and comfort. The specs list an item weight of 0.3 kg (about 10.6 ounces). That's light for an over-ear headphone. Many competitors push 400 grams* (based on available data) or more. The headband is double-layered with cushioning, and the ear cushions are described as "breathable." The earpads are made of a velour-like material, and the listing mentions "longer wearing comfort." This is a headphone you can wear for hours without your ears getting hot or your head feeling clamped. The clamping force is generally light. Which contributes to comfort but also means the headphone isn't going to stay put if you're moving around a lot. On the practical side. It's not designed for jogging or headbanging. The ear cups are steel. Which adds durability, and the cable is detachable and 1.5 meters long. Detachable is a big deal - if the cable breaks, you replace the cable, not the whole headphone. The 1.5m length is fine for a desktop configuration but too short if your source is across the room. There's also a gold-plated 3.5mm to 6.3mm adapter included, which tells you this is aimed at people with audio interfaces or amplifiers that use the larger jack. The design is over-ear. Meaning the pads go around your ears, not on them. That's the right call for long sessions. One notable omission: there's no mention of folding or portability. The listing says "headphone folding features: Over Ear. " which is confusing, but the lack of a folding hinge means these are bulky and not travel-friendly. They're for home use, and the description confirms "indoor use" for the cable length. Now, let's talk about what the data doesn't tell you, because that's just as key. There's no noise cancellation. Which is expected for open-back, but it's worth stating explicitly: the "noise control: none" spec is accurate. There's no microphone, no inline remote, no Bluetooth. In practice. The connectivity technology is listed as "Wired" twice, and the control method is "Touch Control" - that's likely a data entry error, because a wired headphone with no mic and no electronics doesn't have touch controls. Don't rely on that. The water resistance is listed as "Water Resistant. " which is vague and probably refers to the connectors being gold-plated, not that you can wear these in the rain. Treat them as fragile electronics, not outdoor gear. The frequency response and driver size are the real story. And the customer reviews - 4.6 out of 5 stars from over 9,000 ratings - suggest that the real-world performance matches the specs. That's a strong signal, but reviews are subjective, and you should weigh them against your own preferences. The key trade-off to internalize is the open-back nature. You get a spacious. Natural sound, but you also get zero isolation and significant sound leakage. That's not a flaw; it's a design choice. The SHP9500 is not trying to be a jack-of-all-trades. It's a specialist for quiet, focused listening. If you're in a dorm room with a roommate, a busy office, or a noisy commute, these will frustrate you. If you're in a quiet room with a decent audio source, they'll reward you with a level of clarity that's hard to find at this price point. The 50mm drivers and 12 Hz low end mean the bass is present and articulate, but it's not boosted. It's neutral. If you like a V-shaped sound with thumping lows and piercing highs. These will sound flat to you. If you want to hear what the engineer intended. These are closer to that perfect than most consumer headphones. For comparison, the build quality deserves a mention. Steel ear cups and a double-layered headband suggest durability. The lack of a carrying case or folding mechanism reinforces that this is a stay-at-home device. The detachable cable is a genuine advantage for longevity, but the 1.5m length means your source needs to be close. The 3.5mm jack is standard, so any compatible device works, but the adapter to 6.3mm is a nod to audiophile gear, not a necessity. Let's address the "worth considering" question directly. Based on the specs, this is a well-engineered product for a specific use case. The 32-ohm impedance and 101 dB sensitivity mean it's forgiving with weak sources. The open-back design and angled drivers mean it excels at imaging and soundstage. The weight and breathable pads mean comfort for long sessions. The detachable cable and gold-plated connectors mean it's built to last. The trade-offs are clear: no isolation, no portability, no wireless, no mic. If you need any of those. This isn't for you. If you don't. This is a strong candidate. The customer review score of 4.6 out of 5 from over 9,000 people isn't a guarantee, but it's a robust sample size that suggests few buyers feel cheated. One more practical note: the "specific uses for product" field says "Home. " and the cable length reinforces that. This is a desktop headphone. If you're planning to use it with a phone while walking around, the open-back design will leak sound and pick up ambient noise, and the cable will get in the way. In daily use, it's not a portable device. The "antenna location: Music" field is likely a data error, and the "compatible devices" field says "Any audio source with a compatible jack or adapter cable," which is accurate - no special amplification required. The "maximum power input" of 200 mW is a spec that matters only if you're driving it with a capable amp. And even then, it's a fail-safe ceiling.
