# Shure SRH440A Over-Ear: Practical Analysis

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/shure-srh440a-over-ear-practical-analysis/)

I think you're sitting at a desk with a mic in front of you, trying to lay down a vocal take, or maybe you're just editing a podcast in a room with a humming fridge. The Shure SRH440A is the kind of headphone you'd reach for in that moment - a wired. Closed-back, over-ear monitor that's built for work, not for show. Let's go through what this thing actually is, what the specs mean, and where it fits in the crowded field of studio headphones. The SRH440A is a 2022 refresh of Shure's long-running SRH440 model. It's a closed-back. Dynamic driver headphone with a 40-ohm impedance and a frequency response of 10 Hz to 22 kHz. Those numbers tell you a lot right away. For everyday listening. The 40-ohm impedance is a sweet spot for studio use - it's low enough that you can drive it from a laptop headphone jack or a basic audio interface without needing a separate headphone amp, but high enough that it won't pick up excessive noise from cheaper gear. Compared to similar products in this class. Like the Audio-Technica ATH-M40x or the Sony MDR-7506, 40 ohms is right in the middle of the pack. The MDR-7506 sits at 63 ohms, the M40x at 35. So the SRH440A is neither finicky nor fragile about what you plug it into. You can run it off a phone dongle, though it's clearly not designed for that - more on that later. The frequency response of 10 Hz to 22 kHz is wider than the human hearing range, which is standard for studio gear. In real-world use, it's not a spec that tells you how it sounds, just that it can reproduce everything you're capable of hearing, plus a little headroom. Shure claims a "balanced. Detailed sound signature" with "extended frequency response." What that means in practice is that the SRH440A is tuned to be neutral - it's not going to boost bass or sparkle up the treble to make music sound more exciting. That's the point. If you're mixing or recording. You want to hear what's actually on the tape, not what the headphones color in. The sensitivity is rated at 102 dB, which is above average for this category. That means it gets loud without requiring much power, which reinforces the idea that it'll work fine with modest gear. For reference, the MDR-7506 is around 106 dB* (based on available data), the M40x around 98. So the SRH440A sits comfortably in the middle - plenty loud for monitoring. Not so sensitive that you'll hear every hiss from a cheap preamp. Now, the build. The SRH440A weighs 8.96 ounces, which is light for a studio headphone. The MDR-7506 is about 8.1 ounces, the M40x around 8.7. So this is in the same ballpark - light enough for long sessions. And the padded headband and cushioned earcups should distribute that weight reasonably well. For most people. The design is collapsible, which is a practical touch for a studio headphone. You can fold it down and toss it in a bag when you're moving between rooms or taking it to a client's studio. The earcups are closed-back. Which means they isolate you from outside noise passively - no [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)my impression is here, just physical blocking. That's actually preferable for studio work, because passive isolation doesn't color the sound the way [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)my impression is can. It also means sound doesn't leak out. So if you're tracking vocals, the mic won't pick up the headphone bleed. Compared to open-back headphones in the same price range. Like the Sennheiser HD 560S, the SRH440A trades a bit of soundstage width for that isolation. That's a fair trade if you're recording. But a loss if you're just sitting on a couch listening for pleasure. The cable is detachable and locking, which is a big deal for durability. Studio cables take abuse - they get stepped on, yanked, wrapped too tight. A locking connector means the cable won't pull out accidentally during a take. And if the cable does eventually die, you can replace it without sending the whole headphone in for repair. That's a thoughtful design choice. And it's above what's typical for this price point. Many budget studio headphones, like the Behringer HPS3000, have fixed cables that are a pain to replace. On the practical side, the SRH440A also comes with a 1/4-inch threaded adapter, so you can plug it into a full-size headphone jack on a mixer or interface without needing to buy an adapter separately. The listing mentions a "retractable" cable aspect, which is likely a mislabel - there's no retractable mechanism on this headphone. The cable is just standard coiled or straight, depending on the version. Don't buy this expecting a retractable cable. What's missing here's worth noting. There's no inline microphone, no Bluetooth, no [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)based on what I saw , no touch controls - the listing mentions "touch" control method and "media control, volume control," but that's almost certainly a data error. For comparison, this is a purely wired, passive headphone. You plug it in, it plays audio. That's it. If you want to take calls or adjust volume from the headphone. You'll need to look elsewhere. The water resistance is rated as "Not Water Resistant," which is fine for studio use but means you shouldn't wear these in the rain or while sweating through a workout. They're not gym headphones. Let's talk about how the SRH440A compares to its direct competitors. Because that's where the value question lives. The Sony MDR-7506 has been the industry standard for decades - it's brighter in the treble. A bit more analytical, and some people find it harsh for long listening sessions. The SRH440A is generally considered smoother and more balanced, with a slightly less aggressive top end. The Audio-Technica ATH-M40x is a direct rival - it has a similar build quality. Similar price, and a detachable cable, but it also has a slightly V-shaped sound signature with a bit more bass and treble emphasis. The SRH440A is more neutral than the M40x. Which makes it better for key listening but less fun for casual music enjoyment. The M40x also comes with two detachable cables in the box, while the SRH440A includes just one plus the adapter. That's a minor point, but worth noting if you're comparing. The customer reviews sit at 4.5 out of 5 stars from 522 ratings, which is solid. That's not a huge sample size, but it's enough to indicate that most buyers are satisfied. The best sellers rank puts it at #639 in Over-Ear Headphones, which is mid-pack - it's not a runaway bestseller, but it's not obscure either. Shure as a brand has a strong reputation in the audio world, and the 2-year warranty is standard for the category. The "built-in media" listing mentions a user guide and the adapter, which matches the what's-in-the-box section. Nothing surprising there. One thing the spec sheet doesn't tell you is how user-friendly these are for extended wear. The listing says "all-day comfort," but that's marketing language. What we know is that it's lightweight, has a padded headband, and cushioned earcups. The clamping force - how tightly it grips your head - is not specified. Based on the design and weight, it's likely moderate, but if you have a larger head or wear glasses, you might find the pressure noticeable after a couple of hours. In daily use, the earcups are described as "rounded" over-ear, which suggests they're not oversized. If you have large ears, they might sit on the pads rather than inside them. That's a guess based on the shape, but it's worth considering if comfort is your top priority. The frequency response range is worth unpacking a bit more. The 10 Hz low end is lower than what many budget headphones offer - the MDR-7506 starts at 5 Hz, the M40x at 15 Hz* (based on available data). So the SRH440A is somewhere in between. In practice, that means it can reproduce deep sub-bass, but it won't rumble the way a bass-boosted consumer headphone would. That's fine for monitoring, because you want to hear bass accurately, not exaggerated. The 22 kHz top end is standard - most people can't hear above 20 kHz anyway, and by the time you're an adult, that number is often lower. So that spec is more about headroom than audible performance. The impedance and power handling are optimized for "seamless device compatibility," which is marketing speak for "it works with most things." In practice, 40 ohms means it'll work with a phone, a laptop, an interface, or a mixer. It won't get as loud as a 250-ohm headphone would on the same gear, but it also doesn't need a dedicated amp. If you're plugging into a high-end interface with a decent headphone output. You'll have plenty of volume headroom. If you're plugging into a phone dongle. You'll get usable volume but not ear-splitting levels. That's fine for monitoring. Less fine if you want to crank it for fun. The collapsible design is a genuine plus. Studio headphones often live in one place. But if you're a traveling musician or you work in multiple rooms, being able to fold the SRH440A flat makes it easy to pack. The carrying case is listed as black, but there's no mention of a hard case or pouch in the box contents - just the headphones, adapter, and user guide. So the "carrying case" might be the collapsible design itself, not a physical case. Don't expect a protective pouch unless you buy one separately. Now. The elephant in the room: what is this headphone actually for? The listing says "recording. Monitoring, mixing, and critical listening." That's accurate. The SRH440A is a tool, not a toy. If you're a musician tracking vocals or guitar, you want a closed-back headphone that doesn't bleed into the mic. For typical buyers, this does that. If you're editing audio and need to hear details without the room interfering, this does that. If you're a podcaster or voice-over artist, this is a solid choice for monitoring your own voice. If you're a casual listener who wants to enjoy music on a commute, this is not the right pick - it's wired, it doesn't isolate as well as [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) headphones, and it's tuned for accuracy, not enjoyment. The 4.5-star rating from over 500 reviewers suggests that most people who bought this knew what they were getting. The low return rate mentioned in the brand data - "customers usually keep items from this brand" - is a soft signal that Shure products tend to meet expectations. But that's aggregate brand data, not specific to this model. Take it as a mild positive, not a guarantee. One thing to flag: the listing repeats the same characteristic bullets multiple times, which is a common data formatting issue and doesn't tell you anything about the product itself. Ignore that. What matters is the core specs: 40 ohms, 10 Hz to 22 kHz, 102 dB sensitivity, closed-back, detachable locking cable, collapsible, 8.96 ounces. Those are the facts that should drive your decision. Compared to similar products at this price point, the SRH440A is above average for build quality and durability, thanks to the locking cable and Shure's reputation for rugged construction. It's average for weight and comfort, slightly above average for sound neutrality. It's below average for versatility - no wireless, no mic, no controls. If you need those features, this isn't the headphone for you. If you don't. The SRH440A is a straightforward, honest piece of gear that does one thing well. Summary: The Shure SRH440A is a wired, closed-back studio headphone built for monitoring and significant listening. For everyday listening, it offers a neutral sound signature, a 40-ohm impedance that works with most gear, a detachable locking cable for durability, and a collapsible design for portability. It's a solid choice for recording, mixing, podcasting, or any task where accurate audio matters more than convenience. It's not for you if you want wireless freedom, [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). Or a fun, bass-heavy sound for casual listening. This is a tool. Not a lifestyle accessory. Who this is for: Musicians, podcasters, voice-over artists, audio editors, and anyone who requires a reliable, neutral-sounding closed-back headphone for studio work. If you're plugging into an interface or mixer and need to hear what's actually on the track without bleed or coloration, this fits. In real-world use, it's also a good pick for someone who wants a durable, repairable headphone with a replaceable cable and a trusted brand name. Who should look elsewhere: Casual listeners who want to enjoy music on a commute or at a desk without wires. People who need [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) for a noisy office or airplane. Anyone who wants a built-in microphone for calls or voice assistants. And if you prefer a more exciting, bass-forward sound for entertainment, the SRH440A's neutrality will feel flat.
