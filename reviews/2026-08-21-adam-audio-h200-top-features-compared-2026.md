The Sonnox plug-in: the real differentiator The included ADAM Audio Headphone Utility plug-in isn't a gimmick. It was built with Sonnox, a company known for professional audio processing tools. In real-world use, the plug-in offers optimization tools and a spatialization aspect that uses crossfeed techniques. Crossfeed is the process of blending a compact amount of the left channel into the right and vice versa, which mimics the way sound reaches both ears from speakers in a room. Without crossfeed. Headphones create a soundstage that's "inside your head" - instruments appear to originate from points within your skull. Crossfeed pushes that image out in front of you. Closer to how you'd hear a pair of monitors. This matters if you mix or produce. Mixing entirely in headphones without crossfeed leads to decisions that sound wrong when you play back on speakers - you tend to make things too wide. Too loud in the center, or too quiet in the extremes. For most people, the H200's plug-in addresses that specific problem. It runs as a plug-in inside your DAW, so it works on the master bus or on individual tracks. It's not a hardware solution; it's software that processes your mix before it hits the headphone output. That means it only works when you're in a DAW that supports plug-ins. If you're listening to music through a media player. The crossfeed capability is unavailable. That's a limitation worth noting. You're not getting a built-in DSP that works everywhere; you're getting a studio tool that lives inside your recording software. The plug-in also includes optimization tools, which likely means EQ correction for the headphones themselves. Many studio headphones have slight frequency response deviations from the ideal flat curve. And correction EQ can tighten that up. The fact that ADAM built this specifically for the H200 means the correction is measured and precise. Not a generic preset. For a mixing engineer, this is a significant value-add. In practice, closed-back headphones reduce ambient noise by roughly 20-30 dB* (based on available data) depending on frequency.
