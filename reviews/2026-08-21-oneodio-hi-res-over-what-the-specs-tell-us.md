The OneOdio Pro-50 is a wired, over-ear headphone aimed squarely at studio monitoring and music production. At its core, it's a uncomplicated tool: a pair of closed-back cans with a 50mm dynamic driver, a 32-ohm impedance, and a frequency response that stretches from 20 Hz to 40 kHz. That last number is marketing fluff for the most part - human hearing tops out around 20 kHz - but the rest of the spec sheet tells a clear story about what this product is and isn't. Size. Weight, and Build The headphones weigh 0.4 kg (about 14 ounces) without the case, which adds another 0.2 kg. That's not featherweight. But it's within the normal range for studio-style headphones. For comparison. Many broadcast and monitoring cans sit between 250 and 400 grams* (based on available data). For everyday listening, the Pro-50 is on the heavier side, and you'll feel it during long sessions, but the protein leather memory foam earcups are designed to distribute that pressure. The enclosure is protein leather over synthetic fabric. Which is standard at this tier. It looks fine. But it won't survive rough handling - protein leather flakes and peels after a couple of years of heavy use, especially in humid climates. The frame folds and the earcups swivel. Which is a practical touch. You can flip the cups flat for single-sided monitoring - a common move for DJs who want to hear the room with one ear - and then fold the whole thing into the included protective case. The case is synthetic fabric, gray-brown, and weighs 0.2 kg on its own. In real-world use. It's not a hard shell, so it'll protect against scratches but not crushing. If you're throwing these in a backpack with books and a laptop, the case is better than nothing, but don't expect armor. Comfort and Isolation The earcups are over-ear. Which means they should sit around your ears rather than on them. The protein leather padding is memory foam, so it conforms to your head shape over time. The clamping force isn't specified. But based on the design and weight, these are likely to feel snug rather than loose. That's decent for isolation - the product lists "sound isolation" as its noise control method, which is passive, not active. There's no noise-canceling circuitry here. What you get is a closed-back design with padded cups that physically block some ambient sound. In a quiet room, that's plenty. On a noisy bus or in an open office with chatter. And it's not water resistant, so don't treat these as outdoor gear. Performance Specs The 50mm neodymium driver is the headline component. Larger drivers can move more air. Which generally translates to better bass response and higher volume handling without distortion. The 32-ohm impedance is low. Which means these will work fine with phones, laptops, and tablets without a dedicated headphone amp. You can plug them into a phone's 3.5mm jack and get reasonable volume. Sensitivity is rated at 110 dB. Which is high - these are easy to drive and will get loud quickly. That's a plus for monitoring where you want headroom, but it also means they can be unforgiving with poorly mastered recordings; you'll hear the flaws. The frequency response is listed as 20 Hz to 40 kHz. For most people, the 40 kHz top end is beyond human hearing, so it's a spec-sheet flex rather than a practical advantage. What matters more is how the mids and highs are tuned, and that's not in the data. Based on the target use case - studio monitoring and mixing - the expectation is a relatively flat response. But OneOdio doesn't publish a frequency response curve. If you're mixing. You'd want to check third-party measurements before trusting these for essential EQ decisions. For casual listening, the tuning is likely to be slightly V-shaped (boosted bass and treble) to make music sound more exciting, but that's an inference, not a spec. Cable and Connectivity The dual-duty cable is the most practical feature here. One cable has a 3.5mm plug on one end and a 6.35mm (quarter-inch) plug on the other, both gold-plated. That means you can go straight from a phone or laptop into a guitar amp. Mixer, or audio interface without hunting for an adapter. The cable is detachable. Which is a capable sign for longevity - if it breaks, you can replace it rather than the whole headphone. The headphone jack on the unit itself accepts both sizes, so you can use either end. That's a thoughtful design for musicians who switch between a keyboard. An amp, and a laptop during a session. The connectivity is strictly wired. There's no Bluetooth, no wireless option, no built-in mic, and no inline remote. The control method is listed as "touch. " which likely refers to the swivel mechanism rather than any capacitive controls - there are no buttons on the cable or the cups based on the data. In everyday use. That covers music production equipment, laptops, desktops, tablets, and cellphones - but only if those devices still have a headphone jack. Modern phones without a 3.5mm port will need a dongle, and the dongle will need to support analog output, which many [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)our analysis shows adapters do. That's a minor hurdle. But worth noting if you're primarily a phone listener. The impedance and sensitivity mean they'll work with any of these sources without an amp, so you won't need extra gear. But the wired-only design and lack of a mic make them less convenient than a pair of wireless earbuds for casual use. You're tethered to your device. The foldable design and included case make it a reasonable travel companion for musicians.
