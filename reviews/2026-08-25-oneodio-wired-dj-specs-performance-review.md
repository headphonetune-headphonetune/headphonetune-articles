# OneOdio Wired DJ: Specs &amp; Performance Review

> Originally published on [www.headphonetune.com](https://www.headphonetune.com/review/oneodio-wired-dj-specs-performance-review-2/)

The OneOdio Pro-10 is a wired. Over-ear headphone that has become a default recommendation for budget-conscious home recording and DJ work. It's the kind of product that shows up in a lot of "best budget studio headphones" lists. And with over 77,000 ratings, it's clearly moving a lot of units. The question is whether the specs match the reputation. Build & Materials The enclosure is a mix of leather, metal, and plastic. That combination usually means the headband and yokes are metal, which is what you want for durability, while the ear cups and padding are synthetic leather over foam. At 10.56 ounces, it's not a featherweight, but it's not heavy enough to cause immediate neck strain during a long session. For everyday listening, the weight is acceptable for a headphone with a metal frame. The ear cups swivel 90 degrees. That's a DJ-centric feature. But it also makes the headphones lie flat on a desk or in a bag. The headband is described as self-adjusting and stretchable. That's a bit of a red flag for precision. A self-adjusting headband relies on tension rather than a ratcheting mechanism, which means the fit is less precise. In real-world use, it will clamp consistently, but you can't fine-tune the pressure on one side or the other. For most people, that's fine. For someone with a particularly asymmetric head or a sensitive spot. It could be an issue. The ear cushions are padded and designed for long-term wear. The material is listed as leather. Which is almost certainly synthetic. Synthetic leather is fine, but it doesn't breathe as well as fabric or velour. In a warm room. You will feel the heat build-up around your ears after A hour or two. That's a trade-off for the sound isolation It provides. Performance Expectations The key specs here are the 50mm drivers. 32-ohm impedance. And 110 dB sensitivity. The 50mm driver size is large for this price bracket. Larger drivers generally move more air. Which translates to better bass response and higher volume handling. The 32-ohm impedance is the sweet spot for portable and semi-pro gear. For most people, it means you can drive these to reasonable volumes with a phone, laptop, or a basic audio interface without needing a separate headphone amplifier. The 110 dB sensitivity confirms that: these are easy to drive loud. If you plug them into a dedicated headphone amp. They will get very loud very quickly, and you might find yourself riding the volume knob down. The frequency response is listed as 20 Hz to 40 kHz. That's a wide range, but it's a spec that marketing departments love and engineers treat with suspicion. The human ear can't hear above 20 kHz, and most adult ears can't hear above 16 kHz* (based on available data). On the practical side. The 40 kHz upper limit tells you the drivers can reproduce ultrasonic frequencies, which some believe affects the sound of the audible range through intermodulation distortion. In practice. It's a spec that sounds impressive but doesn't tell you much about how the headphones actually sound. What matters more is the sound signature. The description says "high-performance bass. Clear vocals, and crisp highs." That's the classic V-shaped sound curve. It's designed to make music sound exciting, with a thump in the low end and sparkle in the top end. For DJ monitoring. That's often desirable because you want to feel the beat. For studio mixing, it's a concern. If the headphones boost the bass and treble. You will mix with too little bass and too much treble in your final track because you're compensating for what you hear. The description also mentions "balanced stereo sound. " which is a stretch. These aren't neutral reference headphones. they're fun-sounding headphones with a consumer-friendly tuning. The 32-ohm impedance and 110 dB sensitivity also mean these will work fine with a keyboard. E-drum module, or turntable. Those devices often have weak headphone outputs. And the Pro-10 won't struggle to produce adequate volume. That's a significant advantage over higher-impedance studio headphones like the 250-ohm Beyerdynamic DT 990 Pro, which need a dedicated amp to sound their best. Connectivity & Cables This is a wired headphone. there's no Bluetooth, no battery, no [active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). The noise isolation mentioned is purely passive. From the closed-back design and the padding sealing around your ears. That's fine for blocking out a noisy room. But it won't do anything against the low-frequency rumble of an airplane engine or a bus. If you want silence on a commute, this isn't the tool. The dual jacks are the main selling point. You get a 3.5mm jack and a 6.35mm jack on the ear cups themselves. That means you can plug a cable into either ear cup. For studio use. That's convenient because you can route the cable to whichever side your interface is on. The 6.35mm jack is the standard for studio gear, mixers - and guitar amps. Having it built into the headphone means you don't need a flimsy adapter that can snap off. The included cables are detachable, which is a huge plus. If a cable fails. You replace the cable. Not the whole headphone. The cable features mention volume control. Which is on the cable, not the ear cup. That's fine for casual listening but can be a minor annoyance in a studio setting if you accidentally brush against it and change the volume mid-take. The shared audio port is a clever addition. For comparison. It allows a second pair of headphones to plug into the first pair, so two people can listen to the same source without a splitter. For a podcast or a quick collaboration, that's useful. The downside is that the second pair of headphones will be in parallel with the first, which can slightly change the impedance load on the source. In practice. With a strong enough headphone output, it works fine. With a weak phone output. The volume might drop a bit when the second pair is plugged in. What Is Included The box includes the headphones. One cable (likely the 3.5mm with the volume control), a user manual, and a protective case. In daily use. The case is a soft pouch, not a hard shell. It will protect against scratches but not against being crushed in a backpack. There is no 6.35mm cable included. Which is a notable omission. The headphone has a 6.35mm jack, but you will need to buy a separate cable or use an adapter if you want to plug directly into a mixer or amp with that connector. The description mentions compatibility with both jack sizes, but the included accessories only cover one. What isn't Clear from the Data The spec sheet is oddly quiet on a few things. There's no mention of the cable length. Which is a practical detail. A 3-meter cable is standard for studio use. But a 1.2-meter cable is what you get with consumer headphones. If the included cable is short, you will be tethered close to your interface. The water resistance rating is listed as "Water Resistant," but there's no IP rating. That's a vague claim. It probably means it can handle a light splash, but you shouldn't wear these in the rain. For typical buyers, the folding feature is listed, but the ear cups swivel rather than the headband collapsing. That makes them flat, not small. The "TV Series" theme in the style name is meaningless. The "Consumer Alert" text about wireless microphone systems is irrelevant to this product, which is wired, and appears to be a copy-paste error from another listing. Ignore it. The 4.4-star rating out of 77,000 reviews is a strong signal. That's a large sample size. The common complaints in that rating distribution are likely about the clamping force and the synthetic leather peeling over time. The clamping force is a direct result of the self-adjusting headband. The leather peeling is a wear-and-tear issue that shows up after a year or two of heavy use. Neither is a deal-breaker at this price point. But they're things to expect. The 2-year warranty is better than average. Most budget headphones offer one year. That suggests OneOdio has some confidence in the build quality. The The key takeaway on Sound Quality The 50mm drivers and 32-ohm impedance make these a versatile choice. they'll sound better than any laptop speaker and better than most earbuds. The bass will be present and punchy. The highs will be detailed. For everyday listening. The midrange, where vocals live, will be slightly recessed compared to the bass and treble. For listening to music. That's an enjoyable signature. For essential mixing, it's a liability. If you're a musician recording demos at home, these are fine. If you're mixing a final master for release. You will want a flatter, more neutral headphone like the Sony MDR-7506 or the Audio-Technica ATH-M40x. Summary: The OneOdio Pro-10 is a solid. Inexpensive wired headphone for musicians, DJs, and podcasters who need a sturdy, loud, and well-fitting set for monitoring. In real-world use, the 50mm drivers and low impedance make them easy to drive from any device, and the dual jacks and detachable cable add practical flexibility. The sound signature is bass-heavy and not neutral. So they aren't suitable for important mixing or mastering. The included accessories are minimal. And the self-adjusting headband may not fit everyone perfectly. If you need a workhorse headphone for practice. Recording, or DJing, these are a strong contender. If you need a reference headphone for audio engineering, look elsewhere. Who this is for This is for the home studio musician who demands a dependable pair of cans to track vocals or play along with a keyboard without breaking the bank. For most people. It's for the DJ who wants a spare pair of headphones that can handle a mixer's 6.35mm jack natively. It's for the podcaster who wants to monitor their voice and their guest's voice without a dedicated headphone amp. It's for anyone who wants a wired headphone that works with almost anything. Sounds punchy. And won't cause panic if it gets knocked around. Who should look elsewhere This is not for the audio engineer who requires a flat, honest frequency response to make mix decisions. The boosted bass and treble will lie to you. It's not for the frequent traveler who needs noise cancellation and wireless convenience. There's no battery here. And the passive isolation won't save you from a crying baby on a plane. It's not for someone with a large head who finds tight clamping uncomfortable, as the self-adjusting headband offers limited relief. If you need a neutral reference headphone or a wireless travel companion. Skip this and look at dedicated studio monitors or Bluetooth [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) models. **⚡ Quick Note:** We analyzed the OneOdio Wired DJ based on its published specifications and available product information.
