They're sport-first earbuds with a few features borrowed from office headsets. And the spec sheet tells a fairly clear story about who they'll work for. This is a spec analysis, not a hands-on review. Everything below comes from the manufacturer's listed features and technical data. What you're actually getting The S75 is an in-ear pair with silicone ear hooks that loop over the top of your ear. For everyday listening. The enclosure is plastic, which keeps weight down but tells you nothing about long-term durability. The color is blue. You get the two earbuds, a charging case, a [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)we noticed cable, and a manual. The case is described in the "what's in the box" section as a wireless charging case, but the charging method listed elsewhere is [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c). So treat "wireless" here as marketing language rather than a confirmed [Qi wireless](https://www.wirelesspowerconsortium.com/) charging pad capability. The listing never explicitly says the case charges on a wireless pad, and it does come with a charging cable. Don't buy these expecting to drop the case on a charging mat without checking that detail yourself. [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) is the current mainstream standard - and it's genuinely a step up from the 5.3 chipsets that dominated budget earbuds for the past couple of years. In practice, 5.4 mostly means better connection stability and slightly more optimized power use rather than dramatically better audio. The listed 15-meter range is roughly double what you'd get from older Bluetooth, so you can leave your phone on a gym bench and walk to the water fountain without the audio cutting out. The claim of 60% more stable signal transmission than 5.3 is a manufacturer figure and not something you can verify from a spec sheet, but the underlying chipset is real and current. Fit and the ear-hook design The ear hooks are the defining feature here. Standard in-ear buds rely on the seal inside your ear canal to stay put. Which works fine until you sweat or move your jaw. Ear hooks shift some of the load to the outer ear, which is why they're common in running and cycling headphones. OYIB describes the weight distribution as a "zero-gravity feel," which is the kind of phrase that means very little until you wear them. What matters more is that the hooks are soft silicone rather than rigid plastic, and that you get three ear tip sizes (small, medium, large). Between the hooks and the tip sizes. There's a reasonable chance of getting a secure fit even if you have small ears, which the listing specifically calls out as a target. The trade-off with any ear-hook design is bulk. These won't disappear into your ear the way a plain bud does, and if you wear glasses or a helmet, the hook and the temple arm of your frames are competing for the same real estate behind your ear. That's worth thinking about if you cycle with a helmet or wear sunglasses while running. Sound and the driver OYIB lists 13mm dynamic drivers with a graphene coating and a three-layer composite diaphragm. Thirteen millimeters is on the larger side for in-ear buds, and larger drivers generally move more air, which is how you get fuller bass without cranking the volume. The "85% more bass" figure is a manufacturer claim with no baseline attached. So ignore the percentage and focus on the driver size and material. Graphene is a light, stiff material, and stiff drivers tend to distort less at high volume than inexpensive paper or basic plastic diaphragms. The realistic expectation for a pair of sport earbuds at this level is a bass-forward tuning that's pleasant for gym music and podcasts but not reference-grade. In real-world use, the listing says "balanced stereo audio," which suggests they haven't gone completely bass-heavy, but you should expect a consumer-friendly sound signature rather than anything analytical. There's no mention of codec support beyond standard Bluetooth audio. No [aptX](https://www.aptx.com/), no [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac), no AAC listed. If you're streaming from an iPhone or a standard Android phone over SBC or AAC, that's fine. If you wanted high-resolution wireless audio. These aren't aimed at you. Calls and the microphone assembly This is where the S75 tries to do double duty. For most people. It has four microphones with ENC, or environmental noise cancellation. ENC is different from [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) ([active noise cancellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)) reduces what you hear. ENC reduces what the person on the other end of the call hears by filtering background noise out of your microphone feed. There's no [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) on these earbuds. So don't expect them to quiet a plane cabin or a noisy train for you. What they should do is make your voice clearer on a call in a coffee shop or on a windy street. And the listed 80% noise reduction figure is the manufacturer's number for that mic processing. On the practical side, the listing calls out Zoom, Teams, and Google Meet compatibility. Those are just Bluetooth audio apps. So any Bluetooth earbuds work with them. What matters is whether the mic array is good enough to keep you intelligible during a meeting, and that's the kind of thing a spec sheet can't tell you. Four mics is a healthy number for this category. And it's a better setup than the single-mic buds you'll find at the bottom of the market, but the actual call quality depends on tuning and placement that the data doesn't cover. Battery life and charging OYIB lists 8 to 10 hours of playback per charge. Plus roughly 40 more hours from the case, for a total of around 50 hours. That's a strong number for sport earbuds. Eight hours per charge covers a full workday, a long flight, or a week of hour-long gym sessions before you need the case. The 40-hour case reserve means you could go a couple of weeks of normal use without plugging in. One number in the technical specs is confusing. Under "battery charge time" it lists 40 hours, which almost certainly refers to the total case capacity rather than how long the case takes to charge. A 40-hour charge time would be absurd. The listing also doesn't state how long a full charge actually takes, only that it's [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) fast charging. That's a gap in the data. The dual LED display is a genuinely useful attribute and worth calling out. It shows the case battery as a percentage from 0 to 100. Plus the battery level of an individual earbud. Most budget earbuds give you vague colored dots or nothing at all. Knowing you have 60% left in the case versus guessing is the difference between remembering to charge before a trip and getting caught with dead buds at the airport. Water resistance [IPX7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) is a serious rating. For comparison, the X means it wasn't tested for dust ingress, and the 7 means the earbuds can survive being submerged in water up to about a meter for 30 minutes* (based on available data). For real-world use. That covers heavy sweat, rain, and the occasional accidental drop in a puddle or a sink. It's more protection than most sport earbuds offer. Many of which top out at [IPX5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) or [IPX6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code). One caveat: [IPX7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code) applies to the earbuds themselves. The charging case isn't typically rated to the same standard - and the listing doesn't specify a rating for the case. Don't rinse the case under a tap. Controls Touch controls handle play, pause, volume, track skipping, calls, and voice assistant activation. The spec calls the sensor "high-precision," which is the kind of claim that only matters if it actually prevents accidental triggers. Touch controls on earbuds are a common frustration point because adjusting the bud or wiping sweat can register as a tap. There's no way to know from the data how well OYIB tuned the sensitivity, but the feature set is complete. Volume control on the earbud itself, rather than only on your phone, is a nice inclusion at this level. Compatibility The earbuds pair with iOS, Android, and Windows over standard Bluetooth, which covers basically every phone, tablet, and laptop made in the last several years. In daily use, the listing says they work with "almost any device that supports Bluetooth connectivity." There's no multipoint pairing mentioned, which means you likely can't have them connected to your laptop and your phone at the same time and switch between them automatically. You'd need to disconnect from one to use the other. That's a common limitation in this category, but worth knowing if you split your day between devices. Latency The 0.1-second latency claim is about a tenth of a second. For music and podcasts, that's imperceptible. For gaming or video, it's low enough that lip-sync issues should be minimal, though Bluetooth audio always carries some delay. If you're doing competitive mobile gaming where every millisecond counts. Wired is still the answer. For casual use, 0.1s is fine. What the data doesn't tell you Several things you'd want to know before buying simply aren't in the specs. The weight of each earbud isn't listed, which matters for an ear-hook design where the load sits on your ear rather than inside the canal. A heavy hook can become uncomfortable over a two-hour session. There's no information on charging time. No codec details, no stated impedance or frequency response, and no multipoint support mentioned. For typical buyers, the durability of the plastic enclosure and the flexibility of the hooks are also unknowns. You can't assess long-term reliability from a spec sheet. The 4.4-star average across nearly 9,826 reviews is a meaningful data point. It's not a perfect score, and a product with that many reviews and a 4.4 average has a real, if small, share of unhappy buyers. Ratings at this volume tend to be reliable, so the general satisfaction level is probably genuine. What the rating doesn't tell you is what specifically people are unhappy about, which is why reading recent reviews before buying is worth the five minutes. Where the S75 makes sense The combination of ear hooks, [IPX7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_Code). And 50 hours of total battery is a coherent package for anyone who exercises regularly. If you run, lift, cycle, or do HIIT and you've been fighting with earbuds that slip or die mid-workout, the design here addresses both problems directly. The 13mm drivers and bass-forward tuning suit the kind of music most people play at the gym. The ENC mics and touch controls cover the work-from-home calls you might take between sessions. It's a generalist sport earbud that tries to be decent at several things rather than excellent at one.
