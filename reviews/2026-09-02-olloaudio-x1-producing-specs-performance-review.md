You're sitting in front of a DAW at 11 PM. Mixing a track, and you can't tell if the snare is too loud or if your room is just lying to you. That's the exact scenario the OLLOAUDIO X1 was built for. These are not lifestyle headphones, not commuting cans, and definitely not something you'll use with your phone on a bus. they're open-back. Studio reference monitors that strap to your head, designed to show you what your mix actually sounds like rather than what your room makes it sound like. Let's start with the large one: the frequency response. The spec sheet claims a variation of ~9.8 dB SPL across 5Hz to 22kHz, measured with an IEC 60318-1 ear simulator. For context. Most consumer headphones have a response that swings 20-30 dB* (based on available data) or more across that range, usually with a large bass bump and a scooped midrange to make everything sound exciting. The X1 keeps that swing down to under 10 dB. That means what you hear is closer to the raw signal. Kick drums won't feel artificially punchy. And hi-hats won't cut your head off. If you're used to commercial headphones, these will sound flat, maybe even dull at first. That's the point. You're not listening for enjoyment; you're listening for flaws. The driver is a 50mm dynamic with a Pu+PET membrane and a patented pattern. That's a standard size for studio cans, but the material choice matters. PET is stiff. Which helps with transient response - the initial attack of a snare or a pluck. The low distortion figures back this up: THD is under 0.05% at 94 dB SPL. And the 2nd harmonic is under 0.02%. In plain terms, even at moderately loud listening levels, the headphones are not adding their own color to the sound. Distortion is what makes budget headphones sound muddy when you turn them up. Here. It's basically absent until you get to genuinely unsafe volumes. Impedance is 32 ohms with a sensitivity of 101 dB SPL @ 1kHz. This is where the X1 differs from a lot of studio gear. Many reference headphones are 250 or 600 ohms and need a dedicated headphone amp to reach usable levels. The X1 will run fine off a laptop headphone jack. An audio interface, or even a smartphone dongle. For everyday listening, the spec sheet explicitly says they work with smart devices. That's a practical advantage if you want to check a mix on the go or just use them with a basic setup. You won't need to spend extra money on amplification to get them to a proper monitoring level. The open-back design is a double-edged sword. It means the back of the driver is exposed, letting air move freely. This gives a more natural soundstage - instruments don't sound like they're crammed inside your skull. In real-world use. It also means they leak sound both ways. People in the same room will hear what you're playing. And you will hear everything around you. There is no noise isolation whatsoever. The spec sheet lists "noise control: None," which is honest. If you need to track vocals with a microphone in the same room, these will bleed into the mic. If your neighbor is mowing the lawn, you'll hear it over the music. For most people. These are for quiet rooms and solo sessions, not shared spaces or noisy environments. Now. The calibration angle. This is what sets the X1 apart from most competitors in the same price range. The headphones come with a plugin powered by Realphones. This plugin delivers different target curves - flat. Spatial, and Harman - and also simulates virtual rooms. The idea is that you can switch between what sounds like a treated control room, a car stereo, or a budget Bluetooth speaker to see how your mix translates. Beyond that, the calibration file is open data. You're not locked into their plugin. You can load the correction curve into any DSP chain that handles it, like RME TotalMix or Neumann's software. That's a meaningful flexibility advantage. If you already have a preferred room correction system or you want to use the curve in a hardware DSP unit, you can do that without paying for or depending on OLLO's software. But let's be clear about what calibration does and doesn't do. On the practical side. It corrects the headphone's response to a target curve. It doesn't fix a subpar mix. It also doesn't make the headphones sound "better" in a subjective sense. What it does is give you a consistent reference. If you mix on the X1 with the flat target. You can be reasonably confident that your mix will translate to other systems because you've removed the headphone's own coloration from the equation. That's valuable for professionals or serious hobbyists who care about translation, not for someone who just wants to enjoy music. For comparison.
