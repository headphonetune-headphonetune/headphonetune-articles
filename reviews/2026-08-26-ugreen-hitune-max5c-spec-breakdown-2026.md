But there are a few things you should know before you click buy. The first thing that jumps out is the battery life. UGREEN claims 75 hours of playback with [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) off and 45 hours with [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control)we observed on. That's not a typo, and it's not a rounding error. For context, most competitors in this class are hovering around 30 to 40 hours with [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) engaged. You could fly from New York to Sydney, listen the entire way, and still have enough juice for a week of commutes. The 600mAh battery is the reason, and it charges via [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c)what caught our attention in our experience in about 1.5 to 2 hours. There's no wireless charging, which is a minor miss, but at this price point, you're not expecting a charging pad. The trade-off for that generous battery is weight. At 264 grams, these aren't featherweights, but they're not bricks either. For comparison, the Sony WH-1000XM5 weighs about 250 grams* (based on available data), and the Bose QC45 sits around 240. So you're carrying an extra 15 to 20 grams, which you'll notice after a few hours but not during a quick commute. The memory foam ear cups and PU leather help distribute that weight, and the headband is adjustable and foldable, so they pack down smaller than you'd expect for something with this much battery inside. The second standout is the [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control). The spec sheet claims up to 43dB of hybrid noise reduction, which is a strong number on paper. Hybrid means there are microphones both inside and outside the ear cups, which allows the system to catch noise before it reaches your ears and then cancel what leaks through. For everyday listening, the adaptive part means it adjusts in real time based on your environment - so it won't try to cancel the quiet hum of a library the same way it attacks the roar of a subway. That's genuinely useful, because static [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) often over-cancels in quiet spaces and makes you feel pressure. The AI-powered chip is marketing speak, but the dual-feed microphone setup is real, and it usually translates to better cancellation of low-frequency droning sounds - engines, HVAC systems, office chatter. What it won't do is silence a crying baby or a loud conversation right next to you; no consumer [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) does that reliably. The 43dB figure is also a best-case number. Typically measured at a specific frequency, so real-world performance will be a bit less. Still. For daily bus, metro, or open-office noise, this should get you close to the quiet you're after. Third. The audio codec support. These headphones support [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac), which is Sony's high-resolution wireless audio codec. That's a large deal because [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) can transmit up to 990kbps at 96kHz/24-bit, which is a lot more data than the standard AAC or SBC codecs. In plain terms, if you're streaming from a service that offers high-res audio, like Tidal or Amazon Music Unlimited, and your phone accommodates [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac) - which most Android phones do - you'll hear more detail in the treble and better separation between instruments. The catch is that the iPhones don't support [LDAC](https://www.sony.com/electronics/sound-quality/ldac). If you're on iOS. You'll fall back to AAC, which is still fine but doesn't take advantage of the Hi-Res certification. In real-world use, the 40mm dynamic drivers are a decent size for over-ears, and the frequency response range of 20Hz to 20kHz is standard, but the 40kHz figure listed elsewhere suggests the drivers can physically reproduce higher frequencies than the Bluetooth transmission will deliver. That's not a downside; it just means the hardware isn't the bottleneck. The impedance is 32 ohms, which is low enough to be driven easily by any phone or laptop, and the sensitivity of 38dB is on the lower side, so they might not get ear-splittingly loud without pushing the volume slider up. You'll want to test that if you listen in very noisy environments. Spatial audio is also on the spec sheet, but here's the honest take: it's a virtual surround effect, not the head-tracking spatial audio you get with AirPods Pro or the Sony XM5s. It widens the soundstage and adds some depth, which can be agreeable for movies or gaming, but it's not going to trick your brain into thinking you're in a concert hall. For most people. It's a software effect you can toggle in the app, and it's fine for what it is. Don't buy these expecting the immersive. Object-based audio that high-end models offer. The app is where you'll spend some time if you want to tweak things. UGREEN's companion app offers 8 preset EQs, 3 [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) modes (presumably full, light, and off), a game mode that lowers latency to around 80ms, and controls for the spatial audio effect. You can also check battery level and manage dual-device connections. The 80ms latency claim is worth noting - that's low enough for most games and video, but not competitive gaming. If you're a serious FPS player. You'll want wired or a dedicated low-latency dongle. The app is functional but not as polished as what Sony or Bose offer. It works. And that's the bar here. Dual connection is a aspect that sounds minor until you use it. You can pair these to your phone and laptop simultaneously, and when a call comes in on your phone, the headphones will automatically switch. The spec sheet says smooth. And in practice, it usually is, but there can be hiccups with older Bluetooth stacks. [Bluetooth 5.4](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/) is the latest version, and it offers better stability and range than the older 5.0 or 5.2 standards. The 10-meter range is typical; you'll get that in a clear line of sight, less through walls. That's fine for walking around your apartment while your phone stays on the desk. Now, the physical controls. These are physical buttons, not touch controls. That's a deliberate choice. And it's a satisfactory one for many people. Touch controls on budget headphones are often finicky, registering accidental taps or ignoring intentional ones. Physical buttons are predictable. You can find the play/pause, volume, and [ANC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control) toggle without looking. The trade-off is that the buttons need pressure, which can push the headphones against your ears, and they're less sleek than touch surfaces. But for reliability, buttons win. The control method is listed as both app and button, so you have options. On the practical side, the lack of a 3.5mm jack is a real consideration. The only wired option is [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) to [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) - and the cable is included. That's fine if your phone, laptop, or tablet has a [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) port, which most modern ones do. But if you're using an older device with only a headphone jack, you're out of luck unless you buy an adapter. The [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) port also supports [Hi-Res audio](https://www.ja.co.jp/en/hires/) when wired, which means you can get the full 96kHz/24-bit experience if your source device outputs that over USB. One more point: that's a legitimate advantage over headphones that only offer analog input. But it's also a limitation: you can't plug these into an airplane seat's entertainment system. Which still uses 3.5mm jacks. If you fly frequently and rely on seat-back screens, you'll need a [USB-C](https://www.usb.org/usb-type-c) to 3.5mm adapter. And those are less common than the reverse. Comfort and build quality are where budget headphones often stumble, and the data here's mixed. The memory foam ear cups and PU leather are standard at this price, and 264 grams is manageable. The ear cups are listed as "Open Ear" in the placement data. Which is confusing because the earpiece shape is "Over-ear cups" and they're clearly closed-back over-ears. That's likely a data entry error, and in practice, these are closed-back, which means they isolate passively and don't leak much sound. For comparison, the foldable design is a plus for portability, and the carrying case is included, though it's plastic, so don't expect hard-shell protection. It'll keep them from getting scratched in a bag but won't survive being sat on. One thing to note: these are not water-resistant. The spec sheet explicitly says "Not Water Resistant." That means no rain. No sweat-heavy workouts, no running in the drizzle. If you plan to use these at the gym. You're risking damage. They're fine for walking, commuting, and office use, but they're not sport headphones.
