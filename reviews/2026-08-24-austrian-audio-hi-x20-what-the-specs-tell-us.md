Performance Expectations The core of these headphones is the proprietary 44mm Hi-X driver. "Hi-X" stands for High Excursion. Which means the driver can move a lot of air without distorting. The frequency response is listed at 12 Hz to 24 kHz. In real-world use, the low end reaching 12 Hz is below the threshold of human hearing (20 Hz), which means the bass extension is basically flat down to the limits of what you can perceive. This isn't a bass-boosted consumer tuning; it's a neutral, extended response aimed at accuracy. Impedance is 25 ohms. That's low enough to be driven by a laptop. Phone, or tablet without a dedicated headphone amp. You won't need a bulky external box to get adequate volume. However, because it's a low-impedance headphone, it's more sensitive to the output impedance of the source. If you plug it into something with a high output impedance (some cheap laptop jacks), you might notice a slight change in the frequency response, usually a bump in the bass. For most modern devices, this is a non-issue, but it's worth knowing if you're a purist. The driver type is dynamic, which is the standard for this category. The 44mm size is slightly larger than the typical 40mm drivers found in many closed-back studio headphones. Larger drivers generally handle transients better and can produce a more effortless sound at higher volumes, though the enclosure design matters just as much. The "Quite Low Noise at All Frequencies" claim, paired with "Balanced Mids and Highs with Clear Bass," points to a tuning philosophy focused on neutrality. For most people. This isn't a headphone that flatters your music with a V-shaped curve. It's designed to show you what's actually in the recording, warts and all. If you're mixing, that's what you want. If you're listening for fun. It might sound a bit dry compared to consumer headphones that emphasize bass and treble. The closed-back design is significant.
